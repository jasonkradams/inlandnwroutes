## Overview

The Cabinet Mountains Wilderness is a special place. When the 1964 Wilderness Preservation Act was signed
into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson, it was one of the original 14 areas included in the act. Wilderness
areas represent our wildest public lands—rare, unique, and belonging to all Americans. Only 3% of our
country's land area is protected as designated wilderness.

Occupying the higher reaches of the northern Cabinet Range southwest of Libby, Montana, the Cabinet
Mountains Wilderness features a narrow line of snow-capped peaks, glacial lakes, icy streams, and cascading
waterfalls running north to south for 40 serpentine miles. Two major north-south ridges divide the north
Cabinets, sending Lake Creek north to the Kootenai River while spilling the Bull River south to the Clark
Fork.

A dramatic vertical mile separates lush stream bottoms from the rocky crest of centrally located Snowshoe
Peak—the apex of the range at 8,738 feet. Contiguous wildlands encircle the designated core. The east face
runs the length of the range in a row of rugged canyons from which the Cabinets get their name (early French
explorers noted that rock formations along the Clark Fork River resembled cabinets or boxes).

## Natural History & Wildlife

Wildlife in this wild, wet land includes wolverine, deer, elk, moose, black bear, and a small, threatened
grizzly bear population. Mountain goats inhabit high elevations around Camp Creek and Goat Rocks, while
bighorn sheep are common near Ibex Peak. The southwest face (McKay and Swamp creeks) provides critical fall
range for mule deer and grizzlies. Mosaics of conifers and hardwoods from a 1910 burn provide forage for
grizzly bears and wintering elk.

Clean, pure water is a hallmark of the wilderness. Past studies rated water from the Cabinets among the top
5% purest in the lower 48 states. A remnant alpine glacier—Blackwell Glacier—resides on the north slope of
Snowshoe Peak, while permanent snowfields lie near Little Ibex Lake and Elephant Peak.

## Recreation & Access

Some 90 percent of Cabinet Wilderness visitors travel on foot, with the remainder riding horses or hiking
with pack stock. Short, steep trails combined with limited forage explain the lower level of stock use. Over
two-thirds of visitors pack into high mountain lakes to fish; most of the 85 lakes contain trout
populations.

Thirty main trails total approximately 94 miles. Most trails are under 5 miles long, penetrating east or
west valleys to subalpine basins. Winter recreation is also popular, with snowshoeing and ski touring
accessing the southern third of the wilderness.

High peaks within the wilderness include:

- Snowshoe Peak – 8,738 ft
- A Peak – 8,634 ft
- Bockman Peak – 8,174 ft
- Elephant Peak – 7,938 ft
- St. Paul Peak – 7,714 ft
- Treasure Mountain – 7,694 ft
- Bald Eagle Peak – 7,655 ft
- Mt. Snowy – 7,618 ft

## Conservation & Heritage

For centuries, the Ktunaxa (Kootenai) tribe lived in the region, hunting, fishing, gathering plants, and
holding ceremonies. Historically, communities like Libby, Troy, Noxon, and Trout Creek relied on timber and
mining. Today, conservation partners including the [Rock Creek Alliance](http://rockcreekalliance.org/) work
alongside local coalitions like the [Kootenai Forest Stakeholders Coalition](http://www.kootenaifuture.org/)
to protect clean water, wild roadless lands, and local economies.

Proposed mining projects (Rock Creek and Montanore) near wilderness boundaries have faced ongoing legal
challenges to protect threatened bull trout and grizzly bear habitat. Expanding the Cabinet Mountains
Wilderness to annex adjacent Inventoried Roadless Areas (East Face, West Face, Galena) remains a key
conservation objective.

## Mountaineering & Ice Climbing

The northeast buttress of A Peak (8,634') features notable winter routes established by Scott Coldiron and
partners:

- **Blackwell Falls** (WI5 M4, 900')
- **Unprotected Four-Play** (AI4+ M6 R, 2,000')

Ascending two-thirds of a 1,400-foot buttress beneath Blackwell Glacier, Blackwell Falls offers continuous
steep ice and mixed climbing.

## Regulations & Wilderness Ethics

- **Food Storage Order**: All food, attractants, and refuse must be stored in a bear-resistant manner. Sleep
  areas must be set up at least 0.5 miles from known animal carcasses.
- **Campfires & Camping**: Camp at least 200 feet from lakeshores and water sources. Use lightweight stoves
  where possible.
- **Waste Disposal**: Dig cat-holes 6–8 inches deep, located at least 200 feet from water and camps.
- **Drones / UAS**: Drones are classified as motorized equipment and are prohibited from taking off,
  landing, or operating within designated Wilderness.
- **Leave No Trace**: Practice Leave No Trace principles to keep this wilderness untrammeled for future
  generations.
