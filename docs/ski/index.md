# Ski History & Avalanche Safety

This page collects five pieces: a world timeline of ski history compiled by FIS (the International Ski
Federation), a regional/competition-history timeline, a timeline of ski *equipment* development, a

history of Mount Spokane's ski area and state park, and an avalanche-safety glossary. Citations are
preserved from the original sources so facts can be checked; some names and dates in the older
material could not be independently verified and are reproduced as found.

## World Ski History: The FIS Timeline

From the International Ski History Association at skiinghistory.org. This list of historical events
was compiled in 2005–07 by a working group of ski historians from several countries, under the
direction of Elisabeth Hussey. It was corrected and expanded before publication in 2008 on the FIS
(Federation International de Ski) website; when the site was later redesigned, the timeline
disappeared, so it's preserved here as a research tool. Where possible, the source is given in
italics so it can be checked.

From 1897 until today, about 250 skis and some poles have been dug up in Norway, Sweden, Finland and
Russia, ranging in age from about 6,000 BC to 600 AD. A major source is Gosta Berg's *"Finds of Skis
from Prehistoric time in Swedish Bogs,"* Stockholm: Generalstabens Litografiska Anstalts Förlag, 1950

— which, as the title makes plain, covers only finds from Sweden. Other Swedish, Finnish, Norwegian
and Russian researchers have also written on bog skis. The list below includes the earliest finds
from each country and others with something special about them; inevitably it is subjective. In
prehistoric times Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia did not exist as political states, but the list
uses 2007 political borders; the same applies to China and, later, Germany, Austria and other
countries whose frontiers have shifted. Historians now use BP ("before present"), but this list keeps
BC and AD. Dating is done by radiocarbon and pollen analysis.

### Prehistory (c. 6300 BC – 200 AD)

**6300–5000 BC** — Oldest skis found in Russia, near Lake Sindor (about 1,200 km northwest of

Moscow), made of hard wood. *(Grigori M. Burov, "Some Mesolithic wooden artefacts from the site of
Vis I in the European North-east of the USSR," in Clive Bonsall (Ed.), "The Mesolithic in Europe,"
papers presented at the Third International Symposium, Edinburgh: John Donald, 1985, 392–395)*

**6,000 BC** — The word "ski" (from *Suksi*) is used in Finland. *(Eino Nikkila 1966 & Eero Niskali

2001)*

**5,100 BC (±150)** — Oldest ski found in Norway, the Vefsn Nordland Ski. *(Information from Steinar

Sorenson, Glomsdal Museum; Karin Berg, "Ski I Norge," Oslo: Aventura, 1993, 14)*

**4,000 BC** — Rock carvings found at Bolommid, Norway. *(Rune Flaten, Ski History Conference 2004)*

**3,343–2939 BC** — Skis found from Drevja. *(Rune Flaten, Ski History Conference 2004)*

**3,300 BC** — The Salla ski (earlier the Kuolajarvi ski) was found in Finland in 1938; originally 180

cm long and circa 15 cm wide. *(Eero Naskali, "On Ancient Skis," 2nd Historical Conference, 2001)*

**3,000 BC** — Bog finds and rock paintings in Russia and Scandinavia prove the use of skis; half a

ski and a pole were found in Latvia, and a ski in the Pskov region.

**2,700 BC** — Two complete skis and a pole from this period were dug out of a bog at Kalvetrask,

Sweden, in 1924. Berg originally dated them to 1300 BC, but carbon dating proved them to be 2700 BC.
*(Jakob Vaage, 12 June 1984)*

**2,500 BC** — The Hoting Ski, Angermanland, dug out of a Swedish bog in 1921. *(Gosta Berg, "Finds of

Skis from Prehistoric Time in Swedish Bogs," Stockholm: Generalstabens Litografiska Anstalts Förlag,
1950; Suomen Museo XXXV, 86–88; Toivo I. Itkonen, "Finlands fornskidor," Pa Skidor, 1937, 72)*

**Circa 2,500 BC** — A carbon-dated rock carving of a skier was made at Rodoy, a Norwegian island

north of the Arctic Circle.

**2,000 BC** — Rock carvings in East Karelia by Lake Aaninen and the Uikujoki river (Early Stone Age)

show several hunters with skis. *(Eero Naskali, Lahti 1990 & Eino Nikkila 1966)*

**1,700–1500 BC** — Bronze Age rock carvings, including a skier, at Aeskove Cultur; also a clay vessel

with an illustration of a skier.

**1,500 BC** — The Riihamaki ski, southern Finland. *(Suomen Museo XXXV, 86–88; Toivo I. Itkonen,

"Finlands Fornskidor," Pa Skidor, 1937; Eero Naskali 1990 & 2001)*

**770 BC (±100)** — The Sysma Ski in Finland, with a curved edge underneath. *(Eero Naskali 1990 &

2001)*

**450 BC (±100)** — The Liperi ski, found in 1897 in eastern Finland, with a flat sole and rounded

boot space. *(Eero Naskali, Lahti 1990)*

**200 BC – 200 AD** — China: first known documentary reference to skiing, from the West Han period.
*(Shang Caizhen et al. (Eds.), "The History of Skiing in China," Wuhan: Chinese Ski Association and
the Cultural and Historical Working Association of the States Sports in China, 1993; in English, Liu
Quilu and Liu Yueye, "Sports on Ice and Snow in Ancient China," in Matti Goksoyr et al. (Eds.),
"Winter Games, Warm Traditions," Lillehammer: ISHPES, 1994, 70–71)*

### Antiquity & Early Middle Ages (542–999)

**542 AD** — Skis lined with badger skin and a pair of ancient bindings found at Mantta. *(Eero

Naskali, "On Ancient Skis," 2nd Ski History Conference, 2001)*

**c. 900** — Skis mentioned in a 16th-century account by Bishop Oddur in Iceland. *(Thorstein

Einarsson, "Winter Sport in Iceland," in Matti Goksoyr, "Winter Games, Warm Traditions," 54)*

**552** — Procopius, in *"The Gothic Wars,"* refers in Greek to Scrithiphini (Finns who glided).

**600** — Written sources of skiing in the north: Procopius, Jordanes, Paulius Diaconus.

**7th–10th century** — Evidence in Chinese writing of skis used by Central Asian people.

**618–907** — The Chinese referred to Mongol-Turkish tribes: *"The wooden-horse Turks are accustomed

to skim over the ice on so-called wooden horses, that is, on sledges (or runners) which they bind to
their feet to run over the ice. And they take poles as supports and push themselves rapidly
forward."* *(Carl Luther, British Ski Year Book, 1952)*

**629** — The Thieh-lo tribe brought tribute, "riding on pieces of wood [to] hunt deer over the ice."
*(Described by Dr. Joseph Needham)*

**780–799** — Paulus Warnefrid of Longobardia describes the Finns as skiing (Skritofinns), as does

Ignatius in his book *"Suomen maatiede"* (The Geography of Finland), H.S. 1891.

**c. 900** — The old Ladoga ski, Russia. *(Eero Naskali & 2nd FIS Ski History Conference, Lahti 2001)*

**950** — Ullr appears as a protector of hunters and fishermen in the Elder Edda. *(Jakob Vaage,

"Milepeler og merkedager giennom 4000 ar," Norske Skiloperer Ostlandet Nord, Oslo: Ranheim, 1955; Rune
Flaten, "Wer war Skigud Ull?," Arbok)*

**995** — Olav Tryvasson boasted that he is "better on skis than other men." *(Snori Sturlason,

Heimskringla: The Olaf Sagas, trans. Samuel Lang, London: Dent, 1915, reprinted 1930)*

### The Middle Ages (1000–1499)

**1000–1200** — The poem "Lemminkainen Skiing after the Elk" describes using skis of different

lengths: left (*lyly*) for gliding, right (*kalhu*) for kicking. *(Eero Naskali, Lahti 1990; Helena
Parviainen 2004)*

**1050** — Rune stones found from Ballingsta. *(Falkner, "Schneesport an Schule")*

**1199** — Battle between Finnmarkers and Danes who used skis. *(Saxo Grammaticus, "History of

Denmark")*

**1206** — The Birkebeiners Thorstein Skevla and Skjervald Skrukka rescued the two-year-old Prince

Hakon Hakonson, heir to the throne, skiing over the Dovre mountains from Lillehammer to Osterdalen.
Since 1932 the "Birkebeiner" race has been run along the supposed route from Rena to Lillehammer.
*("The Saga of King Hakon," c. 1270; remembered best in the 1869 painting by Knut Bergslien)*

**1250** — Kongsspeilet, a Norwegian book of manners, includes a short section on skiing. *(The

King's Mirror, trans. Laurence M. Larson, New York: Twayne, 1917, 103–104)*

### The 1500s–1600s

**1518** — Von Herberstein travelled from Vienna to Moscow and described skiers in his book, *"Rerum

Moskoviticum,"* published in 1556. The skiers carried one stick and had short skis. *(Eino Nikkila

1966)*

**1520** — Rules were promulgated about hunting on skis in Norway. *(Vaage, Skienes Verden, Oslo:

Hjemmenes, 1979, 251)*

**1521–22** — Fenno-Sweden's Kustaa Eeriksson, later King Kustaa Vaasa (Gustav Vasa), led the fight of

the Taala people against the Danish troops, using skis; he also asked many of his warlords to equip
troops with skis. Near Salen, two farmers from Mora helped him ski over 90 km to Mora, founding the
Vasa dynasty to rule Sweden. The Vasaloppet, from Salen to Mora, was founded in 1922 to commemorate
the journey. *(Eino Nikkila 1960, "The Story of the Ski")*

**1535** — Norwegian postal decrees were formulated. *(Vaage, Skienes Verden)*

**1539** — Publication of the Carta Marina (map of Finland/Scandinavia), drawn by Olaus Magnus, a

Swedish Catholic bishop, showing two men and a woman hunting on skis. *(Elfriede R. Knauer, "Die Carta
Marina des Olaus Magnus von 1539," Gottingen: Gratia, 1981)*

**1550s** — The Karelia isthmus was unsettled; a battle broke out in the winter of 1555 in the

district of Kivennapa. Ivan Bibikov, with Finnish ski troops, led a Russian army of 5,000–6,000 men
from the east along the Viipuri road to Kivennapa; bailiff Juhana Matinpoika led 500 peasants,
including ski troops, at the village of Joutselka. The Finns won the battle. *(Eino Nikkila, "The
Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1555** — Olaus Magnus, a Swedish bishop, published his book *"History of the People of

Septentrionalibus."* In 1567 it was published in German at Strasburg; an English translation by Peter

Fisher and Humphrey Higgins, edited by Peter Foote, was published by the Hakluyt Society in 1966.
Twenty editions appeared, translated into French, Italian, German and English, with useful woodcut
illustrations. As people from Central Europe learnt about skiing from these publications, they began
to travel further on skis and write about their journeys; their accounts are mostly in museums. All
over Scandinavia, in the Baltic countries and Russia, soldiers were trained to fight on skis.

**1573** — Herman Fleming attacked Inkerinmaa; some infantry and 900 peasants used skis. *(Eino

Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1578** — *"Sarmatiae Eurpeae Descriptio"* by Alexander Guagnus has drawings of Russian skiers with

skis just over 1 meter long. *(Eino Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**c. 1590** — The Hameenkyro ski found, with a raised footspace. *(Eero Naskali, Lahti 1990)*

**1609–1617** — Ski troops were used for scouting in the Fenno-Swedish battle against Russia.

**1616** — In the Glebovan village peace negotiations, a Finnish warlord, Jaakko de la Gardie, and a

Netherlander, Antonis Goeteeris, describe how the village was guarded by ski troops. *(Eino Nikkila,
"The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1644** — Johannis Scheffer's *"Argentotensis Lapponiae"* (Frankfurt-am-Main), republished in 1673,

then in English in 1674 (as John Shefferus, *"The History of Lapland,"* Oxford) and in German in 1675.

It contains a well-known illustration of skis of unequal length.

**1682** — Second edition of Lapponia by Scheffer, with drawings of hunters on skis of equal length.
*(Eino Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1689** — Janez Vaikard Valvasor of Slovenia described people of the Bloke plateau using wooden

boards to "swerve like snakes" to avoid obstacles when coming down steep, snowy slopes. *(Ales Gucek,
"In the Tracks of Old-time Skiing"; Johannes Weichard Valvasor, Die Ehre des Hertsogthums Krain,
Laibach)* Skade, goddess of skiing, appears in Presten Jonas Ramus, *"Norig Regnum."* *(Rune Flaten,

"Skiguddinnen Skade," Arbok, 2000)*

### The 1700s

**1700–1721** — Great Nordic War: Russians used ski troops when attacking the Karelia Isthmus. *(Eino

Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1708** — *"Voyage vers le Septentrion"* has drawings of Samojedi hunters with short skis, turned up

at both ends. *(Eino Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1722** — Greenlanders pictured on skis. *(Hans Egde)*

**1733** — First rules for military skiing made by Jens Henrik Ernahusen (1688–1752).

**1741** — First depiction of a skier with two poles. *(P. Hogstrom, "Beschreibung von des unter

schwedischer krone gehorigen Lappland," trans. Templin, Stockholm and Leipzig: von Rother, 1748)* The

Governor of Kymenkartano and Nyland, K.J. Stjernstedt, ordered defending ski troops not to hesitate to
attack, but to withdraw and surround the enemy before impact. *(Eino Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski,"
1966)*

**1761** — Waxing mentioned by C. Hals, Elverum.

**1767** — Prizes for military ski competitions.

**1775** — Literary reference to skiing in Germany, in *"Mineralogische Geschichte des sachsischen

Erzgeberge,"* Hamburg, 1775. *(Cited by Fritz Benk, "Geschichte des Skilaufs," PhD thesis, Innsbruck:

Leopold-Franzen Universtat, 1953)* Skiing is again mentioned in the Erzgebirge in 1804, in Kael August

Engel, *"Erdbescheibung von Sachsen,"* 1804. *(Cited in Der Winter VII, January 1933, 106)*

**1779** — Description of skis of unequal length (95 cm short and 160 cm long) with sealskin.
*(Nicolai Jonge, Jordbeskrivelse)*

**1780** — Skiing recorded in Weardale, England, approximately this date. *(Ski Notes & Queries,

December 1970)*

**1790s** — Russian skiers in Alaska. *(M.D. Teben'kov, "Atlas of the Northwest Coasts of America from

the Bering Straits to Cape Corrients and the Aleutian Islands...," compiled when Teben'kov was
Governor of Russian Alaska and published in 1852; cited in Dave Brann, "Russian skiers the first to
make tracks in North America," in E. John B. Allen (Ed.), International Ski History Congress 2002
Collected Papers, New Hartford: ISHA, 2002, 15–16)* Also, a sort of ski was used in the Riesenkopf

area, Silesia. *(Johan Friedrich Zollner, "Briefe uber Schlesien auf eine Reise im Jahre 1791," 2
volumes, Berlin, 1791 and 1792, 11:193)*

**1793–1804** — Johann Christoph Friedrich GutsMuths, philosopher and founder of a new bodybuilding

method in Germany, skied in Thuringia and recommended it in his book, published in 1804, *"Gymnastics
for the Young."* *(Falkner, Formation of Workers Ski Sport in Germany)*

**1797** — Dutch ship's captain Cornelius de Jong wrote of Norwegian ski corps and soldiers jumping,

with illustrations, in his *"Reizen naar de Kaap de Goode Hoop,"* Haarlem, 1802.

### 1800–1849

**1800** — In Telemark, skiing was well known and practised with great skill in different forms; it

spread quickly all over Norway for health and enjoyment. The people from Christiania gradually
developed their own Christiania technique. Early 1800s: first ski-jumping competitions in Telemark, on
jumps made from snow. *(Eino Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1803** — *"The Costume of Russian Empire"* by Miller, published in London, has drawings of an ostjak

hunter with short skis and a stick with a spade tip. *(Eino Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1808–09** — Ski troops fought in the Nordic war between Sweden and Russia over Finland; Olaf Rye

(1791–1849) is recorded as having jumped 15 alen (c. 30 ft) — the start of kept records. Also 1808–09:
Mamiya Rinshu visited Japan and described and drew skiing in northern Hokkaido. *("Hoku-Ezo zusetsu,"
Description of the northern Ezo Hokkaido, Edo, 1855, in Anton Obholzer, "Geschichte des Skis und
Skistockes," Schorndorf bei Stuttgart: Hofmann, 1974, 17–18; sometimes called Rinzo Mamiya)*

**1812** — Napoleon's forces, during their retreat, were harassed by Russians on skis. *(T.I.

Ramenskai, Lyzhnyi vek Rossii, 3rd ed., Moscow: Svetskii Sport, 1998, 15, and L.M. Butin, Lyzhnyi
Sport, Moscow: Academia, 2000, 12 — neither gives sources)*

**1815** — Oscar Wergeland born, Kristiansand, NOR (d. 1895). Norwegian military officer who trained

his troops to ski and wrote two books on skiing: *"Skilober-exercitie Efter Nutidens Stridsmaade,
Skytterlag of Skoler Tilegnet"* (Kristiansand: Steen, 1863) and *"Skilobning, dens Historie og

Kreidgsanvendelse..."* (Christiania: Schibsted, 1865). *(Einar Sunde, "Oscar Wergeland: An Apostle for

Skiing," in John Allen (Ed.), International Ski History Congress 2002)*

**1825** — Sondre Norheim born (10 June – 9 March 1897), Morgedal, NOR. Improved ski and binding

design; introduced Telemark; expert ski jumper. In 1868 won the first ski competition in Christiania,
aged 42. Emigrated to Minnesota, USA, in 1884; died in North Dakota.

**1827** — "Snowshoe" Thompson (1827–1876), born Jon Thorsteinson Rui in Tinn, Norway. He learnt to

ski as a child, emigrating to the US aged 10 with his family; skied in the Midwest, went to California
in 1851 to work briefly as a gold miner, then bought a ranch west of Sacramento and became a mail
carrier in 1855. Already an accomplished Sierra skier who made his own skis, in 1856 he started his
90-mile route from Genoa, Nevada, to Placerville, California. He died in Genoa. *(Dan de Quille,
"Snowshoe Thompson," Territorial Enterprise, is hagiographic; a balanced treatment is Kenneth Bjork,
"Snowshoe Thompson, Fact and Legend," Norwegian-American Studies and Records, 1956, 62–88)*

**1835** — Elias Lonnrot put the Kalevala together from numerous tales, making Leminkainen the

ski-running, elk-hunting hero. *(The Kalevala: Epic Poem of Finland, trans. J.M. Crawford, New York:
Columbian, 1891)*

**1840s** — Glassworkers at Schreiberhau used 1-meter skis for descent only.

**1841** — A skier is reported in Wisconsin, USA. *(Billed-Magazin, 1 May 1869, 172)*

**1850** — In the Giant Mountains, skis about 1 m long and 15 cm wide were used. Sondre Norheim made

heel straps using birch tree roots. First competitions run in Norway about this time.

### 1850–1883

**1851** — Fritz Huitfeldt born, Borgund, NOR (1851–1938) — ski designer and manufacturer, binding

inventor; jumper with tips down; Secretary of the Norwegian Ski Association 1886–1893; organiser of
Huseby competitions; published *"Laerebog i Skilobning."* *(Leif Torgersen lecture, FIS Ski

Historical Conference, 2001)* L.H. Hagen of Christiania sold skis as a business, among many other

items. *(Vaage, Skienes Verden)*

**1852** — First newspaper account of a downhill ski race in California. Ski racing became popular in

the goldmining camps of the Feather River Canyon (Northern Sierra).

**1853** — Skiing reported from Danzig and Stettin. *("En Skifaered i Danzig," Fra Fjeld og Hari,

Christiania Cammermener, 1867, 72–79)*

**1854** — Skiing reported in Australia *(Vaage, Skienes Verden)* — though it seems unlikely to have

been so early. Certainly by 1861 there were many people on skis, as reported in the Sydney Morning
Herald (6 August 1861) and the Yass Courier (10 August 1861).

**1855** — Skiing reported in New Zealand *(Vaage, Skienes Verden)*, though with no supporting

evidence. Mid-19th century: the first races were organised and ski clubs founded in Norway.

**1856** — Mathias Zdarsky born, Kozichowitz, CZE (25 February – 20 June 1940). Founder of Alpine

skiing technique, which he taught at his Lilienfeld school; invented gate competitions; shortened
skis; invented firmer bindings; taught the stem turn on steep slopes using a single pole. Suffered
terrible injuries in an avalanche in 1916. Wrote an important book on the Lilienfeld technique.

**1859** — Bjornstjerne Bjornson wrote *"Der ligger et Land mot den evige Sne."* *(Tor Bomann Larson,

"Den Evige Sne," Oslo: Cappelen, 1993)* Skis first appeared in Switzerland at Sils-Maria and

Silvaplana, made by the smith Pedrun.

**1860** — Wax called "moko," from Chinese laborers who called anything that would "make go" the skis

"moko." *(W. Hughes, Australian Ski Year Book, 1931, 43)* 1860s: Engadine children amused themselves

on barrel-stave skis. Sondre Norheim jumped 30 metres without poles, holding the record for more than
30 years. *(FIS History)* 1860s: skis were used by miners in Durham, England. *("She," T.P.'s Weekly,

12 February 1904, 226)* 1860s: competition was frequent in winter in Norway, and Norwegians who

travelled to work or study in Europe took their skis with them. *(Gerd Falkner)*

**1861** — Fridtjof Nansen (1861–1930), explorer on skis and writer; his book, *"On Skiing Across

Greenland,"* published in 1890 and translated into many languages, had an immense influence on the

spread of skiing in Europe. First newspaper accounts of skiing in the Australian goldcamp Kiandra,
probably by miners from California. *(Yass Courier, 10 August, and Sydney Morning Herald, 6 & 12
August)* Trysil Skytte-og Skiloberforening founded — the first ski club in Norway. *(Vaage, Skienes

Verden)*

**1862** — First ski-jump competition, held in Trysil, NOR.

**1864** — Johannes Badrutt kept St Moritz open in winter and bet his English guests they would find

it as sunny as in summer. He won his bet, and St Moritz became a centre of winter sports.

**1868** *(the year is garbled in the source and reconstructed from context)* — Sondre Norheim

demonstrated Telemark and Christiania turns at Iverslokkan, Norway. *(ISHA)* Konrad Wild brought

Norwegian skis to Mitlodi, Glarus. *(Reported in Aftenbladet, 10 February)* The Deutsche Turnerschaft

(German Gymnasts Federation) was founded.

**1870** — Sondre Norheim introduced the Telemark ski at Christiania. Skiing reported from the Otago

goldfields in New Zealand. *(Warburton, "Early New Zealand Ski-ing," The Australian and New Zealand
Ski Year Book, 1936; Jakob Vaage, "Norske Ski erobar verden," Oslo: Gyldendal, 1952, 218, citing The
New Zealand Railways Magazine, 1930)* Competition on skis became an important part of life in Norway.

**1871** — An article in Aftenposten (Oslo newspaper) reported that two brothers left Norway in June

to work in Canada. One found work on the railroad between Mt Forest and Kincardine, the other between
Mt Forest and Georgian Bay. In winter they both used skis regularly and "have attracted attention by
the speed they go"; First Nations people and other railroad workers were on snowshoes. This is
considered the first evidence of skiing in Canada. *(E. John B. Allen, Plymouth State College NH USA,
Canadian Journal of History of Sport, Vol. XVII, No. 1, May 1986; Carly S. King, Curator, The Canadian
Ski Museum, Ottawa, ON, Canada)* Also 1871: E.C. Richardson born in Dumbarton, GBR (d. 14 March 1954).

Visited Norway and Sweden in 1894, and Davos in 1903; founded the Davos English Ski Club and the Ski
Club of Great Britain; wrote *Ski Running* with Crichton Somerville and W.R. Rickmers, and *The Ski

Runner* with Henry Hoek.

**1873** — Wilhelm Paulcke born, Leipzig, GER (8 April 1873 – 5 October 1949), Professor of Geology

and Mineralogy, avalanche expert, ski explorer. In 1896 led a party that made the first ascent of the
3,000 m Oberalpstock; in 1897 traversed the Oberland glaciers. Dr Carl Spengler at Davos made limited
experiments on large Lapland hunting skis given to his father. Colonel Georg Bilgeri born, Bregenz,
AUT (11 October – 4 December 1934). Began to ski at Linz aged 20, joined the Tirol Kaiserjager
Regiment three years later, and led his platoon ski-mountaineering in Zillertal three years after
that. He generously taught large classes in Sweden, Switzerland, Turkey, Christiania and Telemark
without charge, and wrote instructions to guides that were captured in war; learnt from Zdarsky but
used two poles. Friend to British skiers and Honorary Member of the Ski Club of GB; died on the
Patscherkofel, on the snow. *(G. Seligman, British Ski Year Book, 1935)* Julius Payer used skis on his

North Polar expedition and introduced them to Vienna. Skis reappeared on the Bohemian side of the
Riesengebirge.

**1875** — In the collection of the Svenska Skidmuseet (Umea, north of Stockholm, Sweden) is a ski

from northern Canada, dating between 1875 and 1877, used by Raatamava in the bush and for hunting.
*(E. John B. Allen, Plymouth State College NH USA, Canadian Journal of History of Sport, Vol. XVII, No.
1, May 1986; Carly S. King, Canadian Ski Museum, Ottawa)*

**1876** — Foundation of the Kiandra Snowshoe Club, Australia (some sources say 1878 or 1880).
*(Percy Hunter, "History of Australian Ski-ing Clubs," Australian Ski Year Book, 1928, 5)*

**1878** — A.E. Nordenskjold (born in Finland) travelled the northeast passage from Europe to the

Bering Sea via northern Siberia. *(Eino Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)* Finland raised its own

army with skis as essential equipment. *(Eino Nikkila, "The Story of the Ski," 1966)*

**1879** — Ivar Holmquist born, SWE; first President of the International Ski Federation, 1924–27,

and President of the Association for Promotion of Skiing & Open Air Activities in Sweden, 1923–1950.
Norwegian Ski Federation founded; competitions were organised at Huseby, drawing about 10,000
spectators. *(Jakob Vaage, Skienes Verden)* Martin Strand founded an industrial ski factory in

Minnesota. First official Finnish ski competition held at Tyrnava on 23 March. *(Lahti Ski Museum)* M.

Duhamel experimented with Swedish skis (acquired from the 1878 Paris Exhibition) in Grenoble. Telemark
peasants visited Christiania; Hemmesvedt jumped 23 m on Huseby Hill. Mr A. Birch, a Norwegian from
Montreal, skied from Montreal to Quebec City on "a pair of patent Norwegian snowshoes" — wooden skis
nine feet long and six inches wide. *(Canadian Illustrated News, 1879; submitted by Carly S. King,
Curator/Director, The Canadian Ski Museum, Ottawa, ON, Canada)*

**1880–1883** — Monks at the St Bernard Pass, especially the Prior Henri Lugon and a monastery

servant, were given skis by a passing Norwegian and experimented with them. Two brothers from Gorlitz
skied on the Peterbaude in the Giant Mountains. Lord Frederick Hamilton introduced skiing in the
Ottawa, ON area, illustrated in a photograph of Archie Gordon on skis at Rideau Hall. *(Collection, The
Canadian Ski Museum, Ottawa, ON; submitted by Carly S. King)* About this time, skiers — mostly

Norwegians studying mining and metallurgy in Clausthal-Zellerfeld — enjoyed skiing in the Harz
Mountains; a Dr Krause brought three pairs of skis from Norway to Hirschberg. *(Falkner, Formation of
Workers Ski Sport in Germany)*

**1881** — John F. Baddely founded the Yukki Ski Club at St Petersberg, the earliest English ski

club. *(Drawing in the Ski Club of Great Britain, Wimbledon, London)* Advertisement for a ski school

in Oslo. *(Aftenbladet, 9 February 1891)*

**1882** — Norske Ski Club founded in Berlin, New Hampshire (an apocryphal version puts this date at

1872). Hickory imported from the USA was used for making skis in Norway. *(Vaage, Skienes Verden,
260)*

**1883** — Wilhelm Paulcke, at school in Davos, experimented with skis procured by a Norwegian

schoolteacher and copied by a local wagon maker; Paulcke was the first skier in central Europe whose
interest persisted. On Nordenskjold's expedition to Greenland, two of his Sami companions, Lars
Tuordas and Anders Rossas, reportedly covered about 467 km in 57 hours; to test whether this was
possible, the 220 km Jokkmokk course was run, which Lars Tuordas won in 21 hours 22 minutes. *("Hier
began Schwedens Skigeschichte," trans. from the Swedish into German, Jokkmokk Museum, 1968; Falkner)*
Cross-country and ski-jumping events were developed at Huseby, with combined scoring across both

disciplines and a cup awarded for a women's race. *(Jakob Vaage, Skienes Verden)* 1883–85: German

pioneers began to explore the mountains, among them Arthur Ullrichs. *(The FIS source list ends here.)*

## Local & Regional Ski History

*Compiled by Morten Lund and Seth Masia.*

About 10,000 years ago: with the retreat of glaciers after the last Ice Age, elk and reindeer begin

migrating northward from Central Asia. Stone Age hunters follow the herds, using snowshoes and skis
when hunting — these implements make the hunters faster over snow than elk and deer can move. About
6,000 years ago: skiing is well established across the Eurasian arctic regions; archaeological
evidence is strong that hunters used skis from Norway to northwestern Russia. 16th century: first
mentions of skiing in European literature, usually referring to the "Scridfinns" ("Skiing Finns," or
Lapps) — the people who called themselves Saami. 18th century: military units across Scandinavia have
organized brigades of ski troops, and hold the first organized military competitions; Russian
trappers bring skis to Alaska.

**1843** — First newspaper reference to an organized cross-country ski race, in Tromso, Norway.

**1840s** — Sondre Norheim, of Morgedal, Telemark, discovers the perfect heel strap — cleverly

entwined shoots of birch tree root, stiff enough to control the ski and steer it, elastic enough to
keep the toe in the toestrap even off a jump — making possible both modern downhill skiing and
ski-jumping.

**1866** — In a competition conducted by the Centralforeigning (Central Ski Association) in the

Norwegian capital Christiania (now Oslo), Sondre Norheim and his fellow Telemarkers demonstrate what
is later called the telemark turn and the Christiania skidded stop turn.

**1868** — Steam trains begin carrying mail, passengers and skiers into the mountains in Europe and

North America.

**1870** — Sondre Norheim popularizes the first modern sidecut ski, the "Telemark ski," setting the

basic pattern followed for a century thereafter — a narrow-waisted ski that flexed more readily when
edged, facilitating turns in soft snow.

**1879** — First North American ski manufacturing undertaken by Norwegian immigrant Martin A. Strand,

in Minnesota.

**1882** — Norske Ski Club, Berlin, New Hampshire — the first modern ski club in America — is

organized by resident Norwegians, and remains the oldest U.S. ski club with a continuous history.

**1890** — Publication of *Paa Ski Over Grønland* by Norwegian explorer Fridtjof Nansen, detailing his

pioneering 1888 traverse of southern Greenland, on skis dragging sledges for 300 miles, using oakwood
skis with three grooves and one long stick for part of the journey, two sticks on the inland ice.

**1896** — Retired schoolteacher Mathias Zdarsky of Lilienfeld, Austria, publishes the first book,
*Lillienfeld Skilaufer Technik,* on the methodical use of the double stem brake and the stem turn,

with one long pole, in Alpine skiing for ascent and descent.

**1905** — National Ski Association founded at Ishpeming, Michigan, with Carl Tellefsen, former jumper

and head of the Ishpeming Ski Club, elected first president, following the first national jumping
championship at Ishpeming.

**1908** — First mechanical ski tow, powered by a water mill, built by Rober Winkelhalder at his hotel

in Germany's Schwarzwald region.

**1910** — First International Ski Congress held at Christiania, Norway — the forerunner of the

Federation Internationale de Ski, skiing's international ruling body. In January, Johannes Schneider,
the ski guide at the Hotel Post in St. Anton, Austria since 1907 at age 17, created the stem christie
with an up-movement to close the skis in the turn, producing a sliding turn — later extended to all
conditions as the expert turn atop an integrated ski technique running from the double stem brake
(snowplow) through the single stem to the stem christie: the basis of the Arlberg Technique.

**1911** — C.A. Lund, St. Paul, Minnesota, founds a ski factory, later called the Northland Ski

Company; its hickory skis dominated the market for another 30 years. First run of the world's first
downhill classic, the Roberts of Kandahar Cup, over the Plaine Morte Glacier in Montana, Switzerland:
winner, Cecil Hopkinson.

**1918** — Johannes Schneider, returned from war service to the Hotel Post and, having taught

thousands of WWI mountain troops to ski, used that disciplined structure to teach a growing influx of,
mostly Swiss and British, students his new technique.

**1920** — First paid instructor in a U.S. ski school, Norwegian Henrik Jacobsen, hired at the Lake

Placid Club, Lake Placid, N.Y. 1920–24: Hannes Schneider formalized his technique into an
instructional system that became known as the Arlberg Technique.

**1921** — First modern slalom race, the Alpine Ski Challenge Cup, held at Mürren, Switzerland, on Jan

6, after rules set down by Arnold Lunn: winner J.A. Joannides. German documentary filmmaker Arnold
Fanck shows history's first instructional film, *Wunder des Schneeschuhs,* based on the Arlberg System

and demonstrated by Hannes Schneider, with a successful premiere in Freiberg, Germany, Fanck's home
town. In a disagreement with Walter Schuler, proprietor of the Hotel Post, over taking time off from
his ski school to make movies with Dr. Arnold Fanck, Hannes Schneider separated his ski school from
the Hotel Post and became a seminal independent ski school.

**1922** — United States Eastern Amateur Ski Association formed.

**1924** — First Olympic Winter Games held at Chamonix, France, with Nordic ski events only.

Norwegian Thorleif Haug becomes world's first triple-gold-medal winner, winning the 18-km and 50-km
cross-country and Nordic combined (plus bronze in jumping); Norwegians took home 11 of the 12 gold
medals. The International Ski Congress becomes a permanent organization, the Federation Internationale
de Ski (FIS); Col. Ivar Holmquist is named first president.

**1925** — NSA recognizes USEASA as an affiliate, and later recognizes other regional ski

organizations, forming a truly national organization. Hannes Schneider and Dr. Fanck publish *The
Wonders of Skiing,* with stills from the movie as illustration; it sells 100,000 copies in its first

year, becoming the most important and widely read book about skiing in history (translated into
English in 1931).

**1926** — First ski shop in the United States opens in Boston, under owner Oscar Hambro from Norway.

Rudolf Lettner, an amateur mountaineer from Salzburg, patents a segmented steel edge to help skis hold
on icy terrain.

**1927** — On March 8, the first modern downhill race in the United States was run on Mt. Moosilauke,

NH, by the Dartmouth Outing Club, won by Charles N. Proctor of Dartmouth. Otto Schniebs emigrates from
Germany to Waltham, Mass. to become the first Arlberg instructor in the U.S.; becomes coach of the
Harvard team, official instructor for the Appalachian Mountain Club, then history's most successful
college ski team coach beginning in 1930 at Dartmouth College, finally setting up an early ski school
at Lake Placid in 1936.

**1928** — On March 9, the first American slalom, set by Prof. Charles A. Proctor at Dartmouth College

under Arnold Lunn's experimental FIS rules, was won by freshman Bob Baumrucker. Arnold Lunn and Hannes
Schneider organize the first open international alpine combined — the Arlberg-Kandahar, March 31–April
1 at St. Anton, Austria, won by Austrian Benno Leubner. Second Winter Olympics at St. Moritz held
without alpine events, but FIS agrees to let the Ski Club of Great Britain organize FIS-sanctioned
downhill and slalom races; the U.S. Team, captained by Rolf Monsen, does creditably but wins no
medals.

**1929** — Peckett's-on-Sugar Hill ski school, near Franconia, NH, founded by Katharine "Kate" Peckett

to become the first resort-based ski school in the U.S. First ski train in the United States runs
from Boston to Warner, New Hampshire.

**1930** — At the University Winter Games at Davos, Switzerland, Austrian racers use the steel Lettner

edge for the first time, making razor-sharp turns and winning easily; alpine racing is transformed.

**1931** — First FIS World Alpine Championships at Mürren, Switzerland: Swiss racer Walter Prager wins

the downhill and David Zogg wins the slalom; British racer Esme Mackinnon wins both women's downhill
and slalom.

**1932** — Third Olympic Winter Games held at Lake Placid, New York, with downhill and slalom still

excluded. North America's first rope tow is invented by Alex Foster and installed at Shawbridge,
Quebec, powered by a Dodge automobile jacked up on blocks, with a rope looped around a wheel rim.

**1933** — Laminated ski construction patented simultaneously by Ostbye-Splitkein in Norway and

Anderson & Thompson in Seattle. First National Downhill Championship held at Mt. Moosilauke, New
Hampshire, won by Henry (Bem) Woods. The Civilian Conservation Corps, under Pres. Franklin D.
Roosevelt, cuts ski trails; Vermont CCC director, Montpelier's Perry Merrill, cut the first trails on
Mt. Mansfield and became the Father of Vermont Skiing.

**1934** — January 28: first rope tow installed in the U.S., by Bob and Betty Royce, proprietors of

the White Cupboard Inn in Woodstock, VT, modeled on Alec Foster's Shawbridge tow and built by local
mechanic David Dodd; driven by the rear wheel of a Model A Ford. Ernest Constam, a Zurich engineer,
builds the world's first J-bar, debuting in Davos in December (converted to a T-bar in 1936). Mt.
Mansfield Club forms at Stowe, Vt., to encourage use of trails cut on the mountain by the CCC. Limited
production of a solid aluminum ski by M. Vicky in France.

**1935** — First U.S. National Downhill and Slalom championships at Mt. Rainier, Washington; open

combined won by Austrian Hannes Schroll, amateur combined by Dick Durrance. First Kandahar cable
binding, holding the skier's heel to the ski, introduced. First overhead cable lift, a J-bar, built at
Oak Hill in Hanover, New Hampshire, by the Dartmouth Outing Club. First U.S. Winter Sports Show opens
in the Boston Garden. First European T-bar: the J-bar at Davos is converted to a T-bar.

**1936** — First issue of Ski magazine published in Seattle by Alf Nydin. First Arlberg ski school at

Mt. Mansfield, with the arrival of Sepp Ruschp from Austria, teaching on a rope tow set up on the Toll
House slope. Sun Valley, built by Averell Harriman as a Union Pacific project, opens with the world's
first chairlifts, on Dollar and Proctor hills. The Third Winter Games hold the world's first Olympic
alpine events, a downhill/slalom combined, at Garmisch; Birger Ruud of Norway wins the downhill of the
combined using his jumping skis. Benno Rybizka, a top instructor from Hannes Schneider's St. Anton ski
school, brought to Jackson, NH, by Carroll Reed to head up Reed's new Eastern Slope Ski School. Ski
survey of the Aspen-Ashcroft area by Andre Roche, Ted Ryan and Billy Fiske leads to the founding of the
Roaring Fork Winter Sport Club, later the Aspen Ski Club.

**1937** — First chairlift installed in the East, at Belknap, New Hampshire. Dick Durrance wins the

first Harriman Cup race at Sun Valley.

**1938** — Mt. Tremblant, Quebec, opens in February with the first Canadian chairlift, built by

Joseph Ryan. First certification examination of ski instructors held at Woodstock, Vermont; Sepp
Ruschp becomes the first certified instructor in the United States. America's second funicular, the
Skimobile, installed on Mt. Cranmore, New Hampshire; the first Aerial Tramway in the United States is
installed at Cannon Mountain, Franconia, New Hampshire. First U.S. Ski Patrol established at Stowe,
under Minot Dole as chairman of the national committee. Chairlift opens at Alta, Utah, constructed
from mining hoist parts. Dave McCoy sets up a rope tow at Mammoth Mt., California.

**1939** — Hannes Schneider arrives in the United States and takes over leadership of the ski school

at Mt. Cranmore, developing the first groomed slope by clearing the south slope of Mt. Cranmore. Otto
Lang presents the first American theatrical-release ski film, *Ski Flight,* at Radio City Music Hall,

on the same bill as the premiere of *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs.* Hjalmar Hvam invents the

world's first useful release binding. Sugar Bowl, in Norden, California, opened by John Wiley and
Hannes Schroll.

**1940** — Alta Lodge is built by the Denver and Rio Grande. T-bar, considered the first in America,

installed at Pico Peak, Vermont. Second single chair in the East installed at Stowe, Vt. Friedl
Pfeifer installs three chairlifts to open Mt. Baldy at Sun Valley.

**1941** — Big Bromley opens under Fred Pabst in Vermont, with two J-bars. Fred Willoughby leads the

Aspen Ski Club in cutting the country's most expert trail, Roch Run, part way up Aspen Mt., after a
design by Andre Roch.

**1942** — Tenth Mountain Division activated at Camp Hale, Pando, Colorado; the National Ski Patrol,

named an official recruiting organization, rounds up 2,000 volunteers in two months.

**1945** — Friedl Pfeifer meets with the Aspen City Council and outlines a plan for creating a top

international resort at Aspen; later opens the Friedl Pfeifer Ski School with partners Percy Rideout
and John Litchfield.

**1946** — Aspen Skiing Corporation formed under Walter Paepcke, replacing Pfeifer's original Aspen

Ski Company; Chair #1 opens Dec. 14/15. First Pomalift developed in Europe by Jean Pomalgalski. P-Tex
base invented by the Swiss firm Muller and Co.

**1948** — Ski magazine evolves out of a merger of Ski Illustrated, Ski News and Western Skiing,

published by William T. Eldred. First chairlift in the Midwest built at Boyne Mountain. Gretchen
Fraser becomes the first American to win Olympic ski medals — gold in special slalom, silver in the
Alpine combined — on February 5 at the Winter Games in St. Moritz, Switzerland.

**1949** — Mad River Glen, Vermont, and Squaw Valley, California, both open. Howard Head markets the

aluminum Head Standard, the first commercially successful aluminum ski.

**1950** — First U.S. FIS World Championships, at Aspen (alpine), Lake Placid, New York (jumping) and

Rumford, Maine (cross-country).

**1952** — First artificially made snow is made at Grossinger's resort in New York. Andrea Mead

captures gold medals in slalom and giant slalom at the Winter Olympics in Oslo — the first Winter
Olympics at which giant slalom is a separate event.

**1954** — Ski Hall of Fame dedicated at Ishpeming, Michigan.

**1955** — Henke Speed Fit buckle boots appear.

**1957** — The first useful aluminum ski poles are made by Scott.

**1958** — Buddy Werner becomes the first American male to win a major European combined, the

Lauberhorn. First U.S. gondola lift installed at the Wildcat area, New Hampshire. Clif Taylor,
inventor of the Graduated Length Method (GLM), graduates his first pupil, Ann Hedges, on 100 cm skis.
Sugarbush opens in Warren, Vermont. Buttermilk and Aspen Highlands open on Thanksgiving, making Aspen
the country's largest ski resort.

**1960** — Eighth Olympic Winter Games at Squaw Valley, California, the first alpine Games in the

U.S.; Penny Pitou wins silver in downhill and giant slalom, Betsy Snite silver in slalom; France's
Jean Vuarnet wins men's downhill on metal skis. Kneissl, Sailer and Plymold market the first
commercially successful fiberglass skis.

**1961** — Ski Industries of America (SIA) opens New York City offices. Instructors form Professional

Ski Instructors of America (PSIA) in Whitefish, Montana, under Bill Lash.

**1962** — Chuck Ferries becomes the first American to win a European classic gate race, the

Hahnenkamm slalom.

**1963** — First U.S. resort association, the National Ski Areas Association (NSAA), founded.

**1964** — Billy Kidd and Jimmy Heuga become the first American men to win Olympic alpine skiing

medals, placing second and third in slalom at the ninth Winter Olympics in Innsbruck, Austria; Jean
Saubert ties for second in giant slalom and places third in slalom. The first Lange all-plastic buckle
boots become commercially available.

**1967** — The first World Cup Competitions staged, credited to U.S. Ski Team Coach Bob Beattie,

French Ski Team Coach Honore Bonnet, and French journalist Serge Lang.

**1980** — The 13th Winter Olympics held in Lake Placid, New York, for the second time; Swede Thomas

Wassberg wins the 15-km race by one hundredth of a second, the closest ever margin in cross-country
ski racing.

## Ski Equipment Timeline

*Compiled by Morten Lund and Seth Masia. Painting: Håkon Håkonsson, the two-year-old future king of
Norway, being taken from Lillehammer to Østerdalen in 1206 — the intrepid Birkebeiner skiers are
Torstein Skevla and Skjervald Skrukka, painted in 1869 by Knud Larsen Bergslien (1827–1908).*

**Prehistory** — Rock paintings and skis preserved in bogs show that hunters and trappers used skis

at least 5,000 years ago, but skis are even older: as glaciers retreated, Stone Age hunters followed
reindeer and elk herds from central Asia's Altai region, using skis covered with fur that worked like
modern climbing skins. Skis came to be used across the Eurasian arctic regions. Early modern period:
skis were in regular use by Scandinavian farmers, hunters and warriors throughout the Middle Ages; by
the 18th century, units of the Swedish Army trained and competed on skis.

**Before 1840** — The cambered ski is developed by woodcarvers in the province of Telemark, Norway.

The bow-shape cambered ski arches up toward the center to distribute the skier's weight more evenly
along the ski's length. Before this, skis had to be thick to glide without bowing downward and
sinking into the snow, leaving the skier constantly climbing out of a hole made by their own weight.
Camber allowed a thinner, lighter ski that did not sink at the middle; the thin, cambered ski floated
more easily over soft snow, flexed more easily to absorb the shock of bumps, and maneuvered more
easily because it was lighter. In a parallel development, skimakers learned that sidecut enabled more
agile turning.

**1868** — Sondre Norheim demonstrates the Telemark ski, with a sidecut that narrowed the ski

underfoot while the tip and tail remained wider. Like camber, the sidecut produced a ski that flexed
more easily when tipped on edge, so its edge followed the shape of the turn instead of skidding
sideways. He also popularized a stiffer binding that held the heel centered over the ski when turning.
Norheim and his friends improved the ski as they developed the first dynamic downhill turns, from
1850 to 1900.

**1882** — Most high-quality European skis were made of strong, springy ash; in 1882, the first

hickory skis were produced in Norway. Hickory is so hard and tough it was difficult to work with
traditional hand tools, but with modern carbon-steel tools, Norwegian ski makers began turning out
hickory skis — a thinner, more flexible, stronger ski with a hard base less likely to gouge or scar.
Hickory was imported at great expense from Louisiana; Norwegian immigrants in Wisconsin and Minnesota
quickly found that, with easier access to lumber, they could make excellent hickory skis more cheaply
than their friends back home. By 1887 several Norwegian skimakers, like the Hemmestveit brothers, had
relocated to the U.S.

**1893** — The first two-layer laminated ski is built by H.M. Christiansen in Norway, using a tough

hickory or ash base with a lighter body of spruce or basswood — lighter, springier, and using less
expensive hardwood. But the flexible hide glues then in use were not strictly waterproof, so the skis
tended to delaminate after a few days' hard use. Meanwhile, in Glarus, Switzerland, carpenter Melchior
Jacober launches what is apparently the first ski factory in Central Europe.

**1905** — An alpine unit of the French Army undertakes the first series production of a

Telemark-style ski in France, at Briancon.

**1926** — The segmented steel edge, invented by part-time mountaineer Rudolph Lettner of Salzburg,

Austria, gives skis much better grip on hard snow while still allowing the wood to flex naturally.
However, the segments had to be screwed into the ski and tended to come loose, or break in two —
skiers usually carried spare edge segments, a screwdriver, screws and glue for field repairs.

**1928** — Swiss ski racer Guido Reuge invents the Kandahar binding, using a spring-loaded cable to

hold the heel down for alpine skiing. A solid aluminum ski is prototyped in France.

**1932** — The first successful three-layer laminated skis are invented independently by Bjørn

Ullevoldsaeter in Norway and George Aaland in Seattle. Made with waterproof casein glues, they did not
delaminate easily and lasted much longer; vertically laminated cores proved lighter, livelier and
stronger, and sales took off. These were marketed under the Splitkein ("split-cane") label in Norway
and as Anderson & Thompson skis in the U.S.

**1934** — Limited production of a solid aluminum ski by Joseph Vicky in France.

**1936** — Aluminum ski poles reach mass production in Saint-Ouen, France.

**1937** — R.E.D. Clark of Cambridge, England, develops the formaldehyde-based adhesive Aerolite to

hold airplanes together — used, for instance, in the all-wood de Havilland Mosquito bomber. In 1941 he
creates Redux, used to bond aluminum and other impervious metals.

**1944** — Cellulix, the first cellulose plastic bottom, is made to go on Dynamic skis in France.

**1945** — The Vought-Sikorsky aircraft company uses Redux glue to create Metalite, an aluminum/plywood

sandwich for airplane skins. Three Chance-Vought engineers, Wayne Pierce, David Richey and Arthur
Hunt, use the process to build an aluminum-laminate ski with a wood core; about a thousand pairs of
the Truflex ski are made, but when aircraft production picks back up the company drops the project. It
was the first mass-produced aluminum ski — more easily flexed than a wood ski, less easily broken,
scarred or warped.

**1946** — The Gomme ski is produced by furniture-maker Donald Gomme in England: a laminated wood core

sandwiched between two plastic top layers and a bottom metal layer, with a wood veneer sole to hold
wax — the first ski to use three different layered materials. Gomme-equipped racers failed to impress
at the 1948 Olympics, and Gomme returned to making furniture.

**1947** — Pierce, Richey and Hunt found TEY Manufacturing to produce the aluminum Alu 60, a hollow

aluminum ski bonded with Redux adhesive; renamed Aluflex in 1948, its second year of production, TEY
shipped 12,000 pairs, but the undamped ski was nearly unskiable on hard snow and the patent was sold
off. TEY instead developed the first snowmaking gun, an immediate commercial success. In 1955 the
Aluflex patent was duplicated in Switzerland and, with the addition of a wood core, the design evolved
into the Dynastar MV2. Also in 1947: Howard Head, another aircraft engineer, created an aluminum
sandwich ski with a lightweight plasticized-paper honeycomb core — too light to track well and prone
to breaking when flexed, but a working prototype for the later, successful Heads.

**1948** — TEY Tape, a self-adhesive cellulose plastic running surface, invented by the TEY trio,

adhered to metal or wood skis but was soft and easily ripped. Chris Hoerle of Torrington, Connecticut,
created the stainless steel Chris ski, the first ski with a continuous, low-drag, integral steel edge
— an edge quickly adopted by Head.

**1949** — Howard Head's plywood-core, pressure-bonded aluminum Head Standard, with a continuous

integral steel edge, began its journey toward becoming the most commercially successful early metal
ski; the secret to its success was Bostik, a flexible contact cement that let the different layers
shear against each other without weakening. Head skis, with competitors and imitators, supplanted at
least half the wood skis on the market by 1960.

**1952** — The first fiberglass-reinforced plastic ski, the Bud Phillips Ski, was not satisfactory

enough to endure — nor were the Holley Ski (Dan Holley, Detroit) or Dynaglass ski (Dale Boison), both
introduced in 1955 — but these early attempts spread the idea that a fiberglass ski might be lighter
and easier to turn than the best metal skis.

**1954** — The first polyethylene base is introduced in Austria by Kofler; Kofix proved slippery

enough in most conditions to eliminate the need for wax, and easy to repair by melting more
polyethylene into scratches. A similar material, marketed as P-tex by InterMontana in Switzerland,
was widely adopted by ski factories, supplanting earlier plastic bases like Cellulix; Howard Head
introduced the final version of the Head Standard with a polyethylene base. Also in 1954: Emile
Allais, the pre-war world alpine champion, returns from five years working in North and South
America, carrying several pairs of Head skis, and convinces Laurent Boix-Vives, new owner of
Rossignol, to build the aluminum Metallais and Allais 60 skis, which revolutionize downhill racing
beginning in 1959.

**1959** — The first successful plastic fiberglass ski is invented by Fred Langendorf and Art Molnar

in Montreal, marketed under the Toni Sailer label. By 1968, fiberglass had supplanted both wood and
aluminum for slalom racing skis and most recreational skis; aluminum laminates remained important for
high-speed GS and downhill skis, and aluminum/fiberglass compounds proved popular for cruising and
deep powder.

**1970** — First fiberglass cross-country skis introduced by John Lovett of Boulder, Colorado. 1970s:

steady improvement in plastics — prepreg fiberglass construction proves efficient but expensive;
S-glass supplants E-glass in wet lay-ups; manufacturers mix in Kevlar, carbon fiber and ceramic fiber
to improve strength, resilience, damping and torsion (or just marketing buzz); sintered polyethylene
begins to supplant extruded polyethylene as a tough, wax-retentive, high-speed base material.

**1989** — Volant skis, the first commercially manufactured steel ski, introduced by Bucky Kashiwa;

the factory fails in 2001 due to high labor costs and production moves to Austria. Some Volant
production equipment is bought by David Goode, who uses it to produce a ski made largely of carbon
fiber.

**1990** — Elan and Kneissl build prototypes of deep-sidecut "shaped" skis, escaping the classic

Telemark geometry toward a generation of easy-carving skis. See also
<https://skiinghistory.org/history/evolution-ski-shape>.

## Mount Spokane Ski History

### Rope Tows, Trams & Chairlifts

The first rope tow was invented in 1908 in the [Black Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Forest),
Germany, by Robert Winterhalder. The first one in North America was installed in 1933 by Alec Foster
at [Shawbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pr%C3%A9vost,_Quebec), in the
[Laurentians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurentides) outside Montreal, Quebec. It was quickly
copied at [Woodstock, Vermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock_(town),_Vermont) in 1934 by Bob
and Betty Royce, proprietors of the White Cupboard Inn; their tow was driven by the rear wheel of a
Ford [Model A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Model_A_(1927%E2%80%931931)). Wallace "Bunny"
Bertram took it over for the second season, improved the operation, renamed it from Ski-Way to Ski
Tow, and eventually moved it to what became the eastern fringe of Vermont's major southern ski areas —
a regional resort still operating today as [Suicide Six](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide_Six).
Their relative simplicity — a car engine, some rope and a few pulleys — made ski tows widespread and
contributed to an explosion of the sport in the United States and Europe: before tows, only people
willing to walk uphill could ski, and suddenly relatively unathletic people could participate. Within
five years, more than 100 tow ropes were operating in North America.

In the 1930s the Selkirk Ski Club, the Spokane Ski Club and the Spokane Mountaineers purchased over
500 acres on Mt. Spokane and constructed rope tows and ski jumps; they later donated this land and the
operation to Mount Spokane State Park.

The first single-chair chairlift in America appeared at Sun Valley in 1936. In 1946 the Riblet Tramway
Company installed an old mining ore tramway on Mt. Spokane — the world's first double chairlift. The
tramway chair handled an hourly capacity of 550 skiers, but it only operated for three seasons; it was
located due south of the current radio towers on the summit, and because of the westerly winds it was
often caked in rime ice. In 1948 the Spokane Mountaineers began construction of a 600-foot rope tow on
the club's 40 acres on Mt. Spokane. By 1950 the new chalet was completed, and in 1962 the Ryker Rope
Tow was lengthened to 1,100 feet. Lodge #1 and Chair #1 were constructed in 1955, and Lodge #2 and
Chair #2 in 1961. The bullwheel from the original 1946 double chairlift can still be seen at the
loading ramp of Chair 1 today.

For more on Mt. Spokane's history, see The Friends of Mount Spokane's book, *Mount Spokane State
Park: A User's Guide,* compiled and written by Cris Currie and other members of the group — [Friends

of Mt. Spokane](http://mountspokane.org/).

### Park History Timeline

    Two source timelines were merged to build this history, and they disagree on a couple of details
    that couldn't be resolved from the material available — noted inline below.

- **19th century** — Long before Mt. Spokane State Park became a haven for winter and summer
  recreation, Native Americans considered the summit of Mt. Spokane an ideal site for spiritual
  pilgrimages.

- **1909–1912** — Frances Cook, owner of the summit, builds a toll road to within 3 miles (4.8 km) of
  the summit *(one source variant says the road came within ¾ mile)*, along with a cabin.

- **1927** — Mt. Spokane State Park is officially dedicated at 1,500 acres (6.1 km²).

### The Quartz Mountain Fire Lookout

The Quartz Mountain fire lookout is available for summer overnight rental, perched at an elevation of
5,129 feet with a view over the Spokane valley and the North Idaho panhandle. It sleeps up to four and
has amenities for a comfortable night's stay; call Central Reservations at
[1-888-226-7688](tel:1-888-226-7688) well ahead of a March 1 booking window, since the season usually
books out quickly.

The 14' x 14' wood-frame lookout was originally built on the summit of Mt. Spokane in 1979 by the
Washington Department of Natural Resources (DNR), on a 40' wood tower, and used for spotting fires
until it was permanently closed in 1994. It marked the end of an era on Mt. Spokane that began in 1934
with the construction of Vista House and its attached fire lookout; an 85' tower lookout built in 1948
collapsed the following winter under heavy snow, a new 45' tower was built in 1950, and that was
replaced in 1963. Over its 60 years of service, the Mt. Spokane lookout held the record for more
reported wildfires than any of the other 657 fire lookouts in Washington — but its structures also had
to endure the worst icing conditions of any lookout in the state, and the DNR eventually decided it
could no longer afford the repairs. Rather than demolish it, the DNR gave the lookout and its
demolition budget to the park at the Advisory Committee's request.

The Advisory Committee decided the lookout would be best used as a rental cabin, but moved to the top
of Quartz Mountain — the rocky summit just southeast of Mt. Spokane, purchased by State Parks in
December 1999 — to get it away from the paved road and busy transmitters on the main summit. The plan
kept the ability to use the structure for fire-spotting by trained personnel during "red flag" summer
conditions, and preserved the historic fire finder for possible reinstallation. Renters make the 2.25
mile trek from the Selkirk Lodge under their own power; since Quartz Mountain sits about 700 feet
lower and the lookout now rests on only a 10' tower, icing problems are expected to be far less
severe, possibly allowing winter use eventually.

Since the DNR had already budgeted funds for the lookout's destruction, it gave the park both the
cabin and the demolition budget. In June 2001, crews removed the 9,000-pound cabin by crane and moved
it by truck to storage, where it stayed until August 2004. It took three years to secure permits, a
business plan, funding, sanitation planning, and a way to move the cabin from the park entrance to the
top of Quartz — Army Air Guard Chinook helicopters, used for past lookout moves, weren't available
because of the war in Iraq. In October 2003 the Parks Commission committed funds to 100 statewide "100
Connections" projects as part of its Centennial 2013 Vision; because the Mt. Spokane Friends Group
committed $5,000 in matching funds for the interior, the lookout project was included among them.

In late August 2004, a state parks maintenance crew made the old road passable for heavy trucks,
poured four concrete pillars, erected the tower, and prepared the site for a CXT vault toilet. When
the toilet was delivered, the same truck and boom crane transported the lookout and lifted it onto the
tower — two sides of the catwalk and roof had been cut off and the windows removed for the trip up the
narrow mountain road, and the boom was stretched to its maximum, but the plan worked and the cabin was
bolted into place. Through September, the crew worked in often-inclement weather to restore the roof,
catwalk and railing, insulate the ceiling, and build an access stairway. Once the basic structure was
up, volunteer campground hosts Jim and Linda Frey — Jim a retired Marine and home builder — took over
finishing the job, aiming to get the cabin closed in before winter. The Freys and other volunteers
(including Bill and Eunice Birk, Cris Currie, Carol Ann Christensen, and Ray Kresek, a retired fire
spotter, firefighter and author of *Fire Lookouts of the Northwest*), along with park staff, pulled out
moldy carpet, cleaned mold off the walls, hand-installed 34 Lexan windows, applied an initial coat of
paint and stain, wrapped the walls in plastic, closed in the stairway, installed lightning protection,
and cleaned up the site — finishing in early October, just before the weather turned colder.

In June 2005, park staff finished the window moldings and caulking, the ceiling, and the interior and
exterior paint; installed a laminate wood floor; hung a door; and brought in a cabinet, chairs, new
screens, a propane stove, bunks, other furnishings, and a 65-gallon plastic tank for cooking and wash
water (commercially bottled spring water is provided for drinking). A picnic table and fire grate with
supplied firewood sit nearby. The nightly fee is $50, with a discount in the first season; call the
park office at [(509) 238-4258](tel:509-238-4258) for reservations, key pickup/drop-off details, and
directions.

### Geology & Native History

The southernmost mountain in the Selkirk Range, Mount Spokane is much older than the Rockies or the
Cascades — the second-oldest land area in Washington — and was once higher than its present elevation;
millennia of erosion and weathering have worn it down and rounded its form. Judging from its shape,
its granites, and the fossil record, the mountain likely had its birth 425 million years ago, and
"before that time, the rocks that formed its crest must have lain deeply buried beneath the surface of
the sea" (McMacken). Although few ethnographic or historic sources record specific aboriginal land
uses tied to Mount Spokane specifically, early accounts describe the hills and mountains north of the
Spokane River as "prime berry and game areas" (Luttrell). Spokane tribal member David C. Wynecoop
includes Mount Spokane on his map of Spokane territory and says his people hunted and gathered berries
there; there is also evidence Indians may have used it for spiritual quests — though no stone cairns
remain today, an 1895 traveler described many such "piles or columns built up as high as chimneys"
(Bell), and reported riding horseback over an Indian trail "just wide enough for one, through dense
tangled underbrush on the mountain side" (Bell). Members of the Confederated Tribes of the Colville
Reservation still gather beargrass and other basket-making materials on Mount Spokane today, another
possible indication of the mountain's long use by Indigenous people.

## Avalanche Awareness

!!! danger "Know before you go"
    The following are a few of the warning signs of unstable snow and possible avalanches:

    - You see an avalanche happen, or evidence of a previous slide.

    - Cracks form in the snow around your feet or skis.

    - The ground feels hollow underfoot.

    - You hear a "whumping" sound as you walk — the snow is settling and a slab might release.

    - Heavy snowfall or rain in the past 24 hours.

    - Significant warming or rapidly increasing temperatures.

    - You see surface patterns made by strong wind — snow may have been transported and deposited in
      dangerous drifts that could release.

### The Avalanche Triangle & Rescue Gear

Know the three factors required for an avalanche:

- **Slope** — avalanches generally occur on slopes steeper than 30 degrees.

- **Snowpack** — recent avalanches, shooting cracks, and "whumpfing" are signs of unstable snow.

- **Trigger** — sometimes it doesn't take much to tip the balance; people, new snow, and wind are
  common triggers.

Before heading out: determine if you are on or below slopes that can avalanche, find out if the snow
is stable, and check your [local avalanche center](http://www.avalanche.org/) for current snowpack
conditions.

Carry — and know how to use — three avalanche safety essentials:

- **Transceiver** — so you can be found if buried by the snow.

- **Shovel** — so you can dig out your partner.

- **Probe** — so you can locate someone who has been buried.

Avalanche survival rates plummet after about 15 minutes for victims who don't die from trauma —
saving your partner is up to you, so practice realistic scenarios beforehand.

### Avalanche Glossary

**Anchors** — Trees, bushes or rocks protruding through the slab that may help hold it in place.

Anchors need to be thick enough, and thickly spaced enough, to be effective; sparse anchors, especially
with a soft slab, have very little effect. Anchors that don't stick up through the weak layer have no
effect — they need to penetrate well into the slab. Spruce and fir trees with branches frozen into the
slab are far more effective anchors than a tree with few low branches, such as an aspen or lodgepole
pine; snow falling off trees also tends to stabilize the snowpack around them. Anchors hold hard slabs
in place much better than soft slabs — like the difference between cardboard and tissue paper when
affixing them to a bulletin board with a thumbtack. Avalanche fracture lines tend to run from anchor to
anchor, since they are stress-concentration points — you stand a better chance of staying on the good
side of a fracture line by standing above a tree instead of below it.

**Aspect** — The compass direction a slope faces. It often takes years of experience in avalanche

terrain to appreciate the importance of aspect with respect to the sun — buy a compass, use it often,
and work on developing an intuitive feel for slope aspect. This influence is strongest at mid
latitudes (roughly 30–55 degrees, from the southern U.S. border to about the northern British Columbia
border); at equatorial latitudes the sun shines nearly straight down on all slopes equally, and at
arctic latitudes the winter sun is too low to matter much and the summer sun circles overhead, shining
on all aspects with similar intensity. At mid latitudes in the northern hemisphere: north-facing slopes
receive very little heat in mid-winter, developing a dramatically different snowpack than south-facing
slopes, which tend to be warmer and often develop thin ice crusts (don't assume these are safer — the
crusts commonly grow weak layers around them from near-surface faceting). East-facing slopes catch sun
only in the colder morning, while west-facing slopes catch the warmer afternoon sun, so east slopes run
colder — and a cold snowpack tends to develop more persistent weak layers (facets, surface hoar) than a
warm one, which is a large part of why the majority of avalanche accidents occur on north- and

east-facing slopes. In wet-snow conditions from strong sun, it's the opposite: south- and west-facing

slopes produce more wet avalanches than shadier ones. During prolonged cloudy or stormy weather, there
is little difference between sunny and shady slopes. In the Southern Hemisphere, all of this reverses.
Slope aspect relative to the *wind* also matters a great deal: slopes lee to the wind (facing away from

it) can get wind-loaded, and cross-loading can wind-load slopes too.

**Avalanche** — A mass of snow sliding, tumbling, or flowing down an inclined surface.

**Avalanche beacon (transceiver)** — An electronic device worn on the body to help find buried

avalanche victims, sending and receiving a 457 kHz radio signal. Everyone in the party wears one,
turned on when leaving the house or car (wear it under your jacket to keep the batteries warm and keep
it from being torn off in a slide). Once on, it transmits a "beep" about once a second; if someone is
buried, the rest of the party switches to receive and follows the signal in, which gets stronger the
closer they get. Range varies between about 40 and 80 meters depending on the brand, though all
beacons work on the same international standard frequency. Beacons only help if you practice with them
regularly — most people don't practice enough, which has kept survivability gains lower than hoped —
but for those who do, they've saved many lives. (About a quarter of avalanche victims die from hitting
trees and rocks on the way down, so beacons can only help the other three-quarters who survive the
ride.) No beacon is objectively "best" — talk to salespeople, read current reviews, and try a few
models, since the technology changes quickly. Whatever beacon you buy, practice, practice, practice:
finding a single beacon in a parking lot is far easier than finding multiple buried beacons in a
realistic scenario. Many mountain areas now have automated or semi-automated beacon-training parks for
single- or multiple-victim, solo or group practice — check with your local avalanche forecast center.

**Avalanche character (problem types)** — Avalanche specialists use nine distinct "characters" to

describe avalanche conditions:

- *Dry Loose* — release of dry, unconsolidated snow, typically within soft surface layers, starting
  at a point and fanning outward as it moves downhill (also called point-release avalanches or
  sluffs).

- *Storm Slab* — release of a cohesive layer of new snow that breaks within the new snow or on the old
  surface; typically lasts a few hours to a few days after snowfall. Storm slabs that form over a
  persistent weak layer (surface hoar, depth hoar, near-surface facets) may become Persistent Slabs.

- *Wind Slab* — release of a cohesive layer formed by wind transporting snow from the upwind side of
  terrain to the downwind side; often smooth, rounded, and sometimes hollow-sounding, ranging from
  soft to hard. Can also develop into a Persistent Slab over a persistent weak layer.

- *Persistent Slab* — release of a cohesive layer in the middle to upper snowpack when the bond to an
  underlying persistent weak layer (surface hoar, depth hoar, near-surface facets, faceted snow)
  breaks. These can keep producing avalanches for days, weeks, or months, making them especially
  dangerous. As more snow and wind build a thicker slab on top, this can develop into a Deep Persistent
  Slab.

- *Deep Persistent Slab* — release of a thick, hard slab when the bond breaks between it and an
  underlying persistent weak layer deep in the snowpack (often depth hoar or facets around a buried
  crust). Hard to trigger but very destructive, can persist for months, and is particularly difficult
  to forecast and manage.

- *Wet Loose* — release of wet, unconsolidated snow or slush, typically within wet surface layers, but
  can quickly gouge into lower layers; starts at a point and fans out like a dry loose avalanche, and
  can trigger slab avalanches in deeper snow.

- *Wet Slab* — release of a cohesive, moist-to-wet layer when liquid water weakens the bond between
  the slab and the surface below; often occurs during prolonged warming or rain-on-snow events, and can
  be very unpredictable and destructive.

- *Cornice Fall* — release of an overhanging mass of snow formed as wind moves snow over a sharp
  terrain feature (like a ridge) and deposits it on the lee side. Cornices range from small soft drifts
  to hard overhangs 30 feet or taller; they can break off suddenly and catch people by surprise even on
  flat ground above the slope, and can entrain loose snow or trigger slab avalanches. Cornices are
  elegant, cantilevered structures — and one of the mountains' most dangerous attractions. Each time the
  wind blows, the cornice extends further outward, so the freshest, most easily triggered snow tends
  to sit right at the exposed edge while the harder, more stable snow forms the root; cornices become
  more unstable during storms (especially windy ones) or during rapid warming and prolonged melting.
  Cornice fall avalanches don't kill many people, but those who do get in trouble almost always trigger
  it themselves by walking too close to the edge — cornices notoriously break farther back than
  expected, so never approach a drop-off edge without first checking it out from a safe place. Cornices
  aren't all bad: professionals intentionally trigger them to test slope stability below, or to create
  an escape route off a ridge (see *Cornice tests*, below).

- *Glide* — release of the entire snow cover as it glides over the ground; can be wet, moist, or
  almost entirely dry. Glide avalanches occur along very specific paths, where the slope is steep
  enough and the ground surface smooth enough, and are often preceded by full-depth glide cracks —
  though the time between a crack appearing and an avalanche releasing can range from seconds to
  months. They're unlikely to be triggered by a person and are nearly impossible to forecast, posing a
  hazard that's extremely difficult to manage. (See *Glide*, below, for more on the mechanism.)

**Avalanche path** — A terrain feature where an avalanche occurs, composed of a starting zone, track,

and runout zone. Forecasters describe the *location* of a given avalanche problem across the terrain

(for example: likely on all high-elevation aspects and north- to west-facing mid elevations, less

likely elsewhere), the *likelihood* of triggering (on a scale from "unlikely" — few avalanches could be

triggered, none expected naturally — to "certain" — humans can trigger avalanches on many slopes,
natural avalanches expected), and the *size* of the potential avalanche(s), a qualitative estimate of

destructive potential: "Small" avalanches aren't large enough to bury a person (though they can carry
someone over a cliff or through trees/rocks); moving up the scale, "Large" avalanches can bury, injure
or kill; "Very Large" avalanches can bury or destroy vehicles or houses; "Historic" avalanches are
massive events capable of altering the landscape.

**Bed surface** — The surface — ground or snow — over which a fracture and subsequent avalanche

release occurs. An avalanche doesn't strictly need a bed surface (sometimes it fractures within a
thick weak layer and creates its own), but common bed surfaces include rain crusts, sun crusts, hard
old snow surfaces, and the ground itself.

**Collapse** — When the fracture of a lower snow layer causes an upper layer to fall; also called a

whumpf, an obvious sign of instability. Collapsing snow (sometimes mistakenly called "settlement") is
the loud "whumpf" of a snowpack failing when your weight breaks the camel's back and catastrophically
collapses a buried weak layer — most commonly faceted snow or surface hoar. Collapsing on a flat valley
bottom can trigger avalanches on steeper slopes above, and the collapse can sometimes propagate very
long distances, triggering avalanches on more distant steep slopes. Not surprisingly, collapsing snow
means the snowpack is extremely unstable, and it's an obvious clue that you need to stay off — and out
from underneath — avalanche terrain.

**Concave slope** — A terrain feature rounded inward like the inside of a bowl (steep to less steep).

On small concave slopes there's sometimes enough compressive support from below to prevent hard slabs
from releasing, but on medium-to-large slopes that support plays very little role. Slope shape (concave,
convex, or planar) makes more difference on smaller slopes than on larger ones, and generally less
difference than people assume — avalanches happen on any steep slope without thick anchors, regardless
of shape.

**Convex slope** — A terrain feature curved like the outside of a sphere (less steep to more steep).

Convex slopes have less compressive support at the bottom, which matters for small avalanche paths,
somewhat for medium ones, and little for large ones — but they statistically produce more avalanches
and avalanche accidents than other slope shapes, both because they're inherently less stable and
because they present more difficult safe-travel problems.

**Corn snow** — Large-grained, rounded crystals formed by repeated melting and freezing. Under corn

snow (melt-freeze) conditions, a crust forms that supports your weight when frozen but turns to deep
slush during the heat of the day. As water percolates through the snowpack it dissolves the bonds
between crystals — the more saturated the snow, the weaker it gets — but wet snow doesn't instantly
avalanche thanks partly to the surface tension of the water itself. Corn snow becomes "ripe" when the
bonds between grains just start to melt, giving a velvety texture good for riding; this usually happens
in the morning, with exact timing dependent on aspect. Too early and the frozen surface can rattle your
fillings; too late, and too many bonds have melted, turning it into dangerous, avalanche-prone slush.

**Cornice** — A mass of snow deposited by the wind, often overhanging, usually near a sharp terrain

break such as a ridge; can break off unexpectedly and should be approached with caution. *(See Cornice
Fall under Avalanche character, above, and Cornice tests, below.)*

**Cornice tests** — Squeamish people might think deliberately triggering a cornice sounds dangerous,

but it's a standard technique among ski patrollers, heli-ski guides, and climbers — cornices are the
"bombs of the backcountry." First, make sure no one is below you. Then find a cornice that weighs
significantly more than a person and knock it down the slope — a cornice the size of a refrigerator or
small car, bouncing down a slope, makes an excellent stability test (smaller cornices are less
effective). You can kick it, shovel it, or cut it with a snow saw mounted on a ski pole; for larger
cornices, a knotted parachute cord thrown or pushed over the edge with a probe works like the teeth of
a saw, and can cut off a fairly large cornice in under five minutes. Work with small, fresh cornices
rather than large, old, hard ones. Heavy rocks trundled down a slope work similarly, though they're
often harder to find — this is also a good way to create a safe descent route in very unstable
conditions (make an avalanche and use the slide path to descend). Caveat: knocking cornices down
avalanche paths can be dangerous — always use a belay rope on slopes with bad consequences, and
practice on safe slopes first. Cornices have a nasty habit of breaking farther back than expected.

**Couloir** — A steep gully in alpine terrain, usually filled with snow bound by rock walls in winter.

Couloirs can help anchor snow to the slope but create serious hazard if an avalanche does occur, and
pose challenges for snowpack evaluation and safe travel — often the only reasonable route is straight
up the couloir, exposing the whole group to danger for extended periods, and approaching from the top
doesn't necessarily make reading the snow conditions inside any easier.

**Cross loading** — Wind blowing across a slope, depositing drifts on the sides of gullies or other

terrain features, at any elevation depending on wind; higher areas are typically scoured, low areas may
look smooth or pillowed.

**Crown face** — The top fracture surface of a slab avalanche, usually smooth, clean-cut, and angled

90 degrees to the bed surface.

**Deep slab avalanche** — An avalanche that breaks deeply into old weak layers of snow formed some

time ago.

**Density, snow** — The mass of snow per unit volume, often expressed as percent water content: new

powder is low density (3–10%), heavy or wet snow more dense (10–20%). Stability depends heavily on the
strength of the weak layer versus the load it must support, which is a factor of the slab's depth and
density. New snowfall starts around 3–20% density; as it accumulates, metamorphism gradually makes it
denser, with the rate depending on temperature — warm weather quickly ruins light powder, while cold
temperatures slow densification and preserve it. Storm trends matter too: if temperatures start warm
and turn colder, you get a "right side up" storm — light and fluffy on top, denser with depth. An
"upside down" storm (increasing temperatures during snowfall) leaves heavier, denser snow on top of
lighter snow, which can create a slab over a weak layer — the ingredients for slab avalanches.

**Depth hoar** — Large-grained, faceted, cup-shaped crystals near the ground, formed by large

temperature gradients within the snowpack. As long as the ground has an insulating blanket of snow, it
stays warm — near freezing — even in very cold air (the exceptions being permafrost areas or very thin
snow cover combined with very cold temperatures). The snow surface, meanwhile, can get extremely cold,
especially under a clear sky, creating one of the most common temperature-gradient conditions —
especially in early winter, when cold temperatures combine with a thin snowpack to breed depth hoar
near the ground.
&nbsp;
*Looks like:* sparkly, larger-grained; beginning and intermediate facets are square, 1–3 mm; advanced

facets can be cup-shaped, 4–10 mm. *Feels like:* loose, granular, runs through your fingers, crunchy

underfoot. *Also called:* Temperature Gradient (TG) snow (an outdated term), sugar snow, squares.
*Forms:* from large temperature gradients between the warm ground and cold snow surface, usually

needing a thin snowpack combined with clear sky or cold air; grows best at snow temperatures from -2°C
to -15°C. *Mechanical properties:* behaves like a stack of champagne glasses — relatively stronger in

compression than in shear, fails in both collapse and shear, especially nasty on a hard bed surface,
and commonly propagates long distances, around corners, and can be triggered from the bottom of a
slope. *Persistence:* extremely persistent, from days to weeks depending on temperature — the larger

the grain, the more persistent — and percolating spring meltwater often reactivates large-grained depth
hoar. *Distribution:* at mid latitudes, mainly on shady (NW–NE) aspects; in very cold climates, on

warmer, sun-exposed slopes instead; at arctic and equatorial latitudes, much less aspect preference.
Common throughout the season in continental climates (often making up the entire snowpack until about
February), before about January in intermountain climates, and rare (mostly early season) in maritime
climates. *Routefinding:* easily triggered from the bottom of a slope or an adjacent flat area — pay

attention to what your slope connects to, and avoid rock outcrops in the middle of otherwise shallow
slopes.

**Dry snow avalanche** — An avalanche in dry snow at below-freezing temperatures; can be either sluffs

(loose snow) or slabs. The vast majority of avalanche fatalities are caused by dry slab avalanches.

**Faceted snow** — Angular snow with poor bonding, created by large temperature gradients within the

snowpack. A temperature gradient is how fast temperature changes over a given distance in the
snowpack; because warm air holds more water vapor than cold air, temperature gradients also create
vapor-pressure gradients, and that concentrated water vapor diffuses from high to low concentration.
When it diffuses rapidly, it turns rounded crystals into faceted ones — turning strong snow into weak
snow, which is why forecasters pay so much attention to temperature gradients. The process is fully
reversible: a strong gradient turns rounds into facets, a weak gradient turns facets back into rounds
— but the reverse happens much more slowly, since it takes a lot of energy to form a faceted crystal,
and once the gradient (energy source) is removed, it can take weeks or months for the crystal to
return to equilibrium (rounds), depending on the ambient snow temperature and how much load sits on top.
In cold climates with little load on top, faceted snow may never gain much strength even without an
ongoing gradient. In short: small temperature gradients strengthen snow, large ones weaken it. For an
average snowpack temperature around -5°C, the critical gradient is roughly 1°C per 10 cm; colder snow
(below -10°C) needs a somewhat higher gradient to facet, warmer snow slightly less. Sticking two
thermometers 10 cm apart in a snowpit wall and measuring the difference tells you whether that layer is
gaining strength (small gradient) or losing it (large gradient) — a genuinely useful forecasting tool.

**Fracture** — The process of crack propagation; when it occurs in a layer of snow underneath a slab

on a steep slope, a slab avalanche results. Avalanches don't "strike without warning," as the press
often suggests — they're the visible finale of a long series of precursors that can begin hours or
days earlier, usually when new or wind-blown snow piles weight on a buried weak layer. Within a
stressed weak layer, think of it as a race between bonds breaking and bonds re-forming, at three rates
of deformation: at a *slow* rate, more bonds re-form than break and the layer actually gains strength —

sensitive microphones can even hear individual bonds break, like the sound of slowly ripping Velcro, at
a *medium* rate where roughly as many bonds break as form; at a *rapid* rate, more bonds break than

form and the layer progressively loses strength — what's called "failure" or "strain softening" (like
repeatedly bending a paper clip: after about ten bends it's weaker, after fifteen it snaps). Failure
occurs slowly, at perhaps centimeters per hour; fracture occurs catastrophically, measured anywhere
from 20 to more than 100 meters per second — the slope shatters like glass. When a person triggers an
avalanche, they've found a trigger point: perhaps a spot where the slab is thinner, letting more of
their weight affect the weak layer, or where the weak layer itself is more poorly bonded. Without a
proper trigger, an unstable slope can teeter on the brink for a long time, giving a false sense that
all is well — after a storm, there's no way to know how many slopes would slide if they just had the
right trigger.

**Glide** — Occurs when the entire snowpack slowly slides as a unit on the ground, similar to a

glacier — not to be confused with the catastrophic release of a slab avalanche. Glide is a slow
process, usually over several days, caused by meltwater lubricating the ground and letting the
overlying snowpack "glide" downhill; most of the time it doesn't produce an avalanche, but occasionally
it releases catastrophically as a glide avalanche, so the presence of glide cracks alone doesn't
necessarily mean danger. It's often difficult for a person to trigger a glide avalanche, but it's not
smart to be on top of — or especially camped under — one. Glide avalanches are found mostly in wet
climates, and in dry climates mainly in spring (as meltwater percolates through the snow) or during mid-winter
thaws. Like an icefall, they come down randomly in time — not necessarily during the heat of the day or
peak meltwater flow, and sometimes just as often with the arrival of cold temperatures following a
melt as during the melt itself — making them essentially impossible to forecast. Just don't spend much
time underneath them.

**Graupel** — Heavily rimed new snow, often shaped like tiny styrofoam balls, that stings your face

when it falls. It forms from strong convective activity within a storm — upward vertical motion caused
by a passing cold front or springtime convective showers — and is sometimes accompanied by lightning.
Graupel is a common, short-lived weak layer in maritime climates, rarer in continental ones; it's
tricky because it tends to roll off cliffs and steep terrain and collect on gentler slopes below, so
climbers and extreme riders sometimes trigger graupel avalanches just after descending steep terrain
and arriving on gentler slopes below, when they're starting to relax. Graupel weak layers commonly
stabilize within a day or two after a storm, depending on temperature.

**Hard slab avalanche** — A slab avalanche of hard, dense snow (typically at least 300 kg/m³) —

stiff, cohesive slabs usually deposited by strong wind drifting, or old, hardened layers. Think of them
like a pane of glass on top of potato chips: harder to trigger than soft slabs, but they tend to
propagate farther and produce a larger, more dangerous avalanche, with the fracture line forming
farther above you and escape becoming harder. Hard slabs are especially tricky because stiffness and
thickness vary a lot from place to place — you might not be able to trigger one in a thick spot, but
you may trigger the whole slope near the edge, where it thins out (for instance, near a ridgeline).

**Isothermal** — When all layers of the snowpack are at the same temperature, typically at the

freezing point — often a snowpack that's wet throughout its depth. Occurs in spring, or after many
freeze-thaw cycles.

**Layer, snow** — A snowpack stratum differentiated from others by weather, metamorphism, or other

processes. Snow storms vary in wind, temperature and other factors, creating a layered snowpack; over
time the snow settles and changes further, differentiating the layers still more. The result is a
snowpack made of layers with varying hardness, strength and cohesion — the relationship between layers
is a basic element of avalanche forecasting.

**Leeward** — The downwind side of an obstacle such as a ridge. Wind erodes snow from the windward

(upwind) side and deposits it on the leeward (downwind) side; deposited snow looks smooth and rounded.
Always be wary of recent wind-drifted snow deposits on steep slopes.

**Loading** — Adding weight on top of a snowpack, usually from precipitation, wind drifting, or a

person. Wind is by far the fastest way to load snow onto a slope — it can deposit snow ten times faster
than snow falling from the sky, eroding from the windward side and depositing smooth, rounded snow on
the leeward side. The second-fastest way is new snow or rain: rapidly added weight almost always means
rapidly rising avalanche danger, and precipitation usually falls more at higher elevations and on the
windward side of mountain ranges (with wind drifting near ridges being the exception).

**Loose snow avalanche (point-release)** — An avalanche that releases from a point and spreads

downhill, collecting more snow as it goes — different from a slab avalanche, and also called a
point-release or sluff. Small loose snow avalanches are called sluffs; loose snow avalanches can be
either dry or wet. Because they tend to be small and fracture below you as you cross a slope, rather
than above you like slab avalanches, very few people are killed by them, and avalanche culture tends to
minimize the danger, sometimes calling them "harmless sluffs" — which isn't always the case. Houses
have been destroyed by "harmless sluffs," and if caught in one you can be carried over cliffs, into
crevasses, or buried deeply in a terrain trap such as a gully; most people killed in sluffs are climbers
caught in naturally triggered sluffs descending from above, especially in wet or springtime conditions.
Wet loose snow slides, made of dense, heavy snow, can occasionally grow to large and destructive sizes.
On the other hand, sluffs can be a sign of stability within the deeper snowpack, when new snow sluffs
off without triggering deeper slabs — they're usually easy to deal with, unlike slabs.

**Metamorphism, snow** — The physical change of snow grains within the snowpack due to differences in

temperature and pressure. From the moment it lands, snow begins an endless process of metamorphism —
few substances in nature change so dramatically and quickly, since water is one of the only naturally
occurring substances that exists near its "triple point," where solid, liquid and vapor phases can all
be present at once. Small, subtle changes in temperature, pressure and humidity gradient can have a
dramatic effect on the crystal that forms. Common types:

- *New snow* (powder, rime, graupel, etc.) — no two crystals alike; on the snow surface; falls from the
  sky.

- *Rounded snow* (equilibrium snow, old snow) — fine-grained, chalky; found in old layers; forms under
  low temperature-gradient conditions (typically under 1°C per 10 cm).

- *Faceted snow* (sugar snow, kinetic snow; depth hoar when near the ground) — sparkly, large-grained;
  found anywhere in the snowpack; forms under large temperature-gradient conditions (typically over
  1°C per 10 cm).

- *Surface hoar* (frost, feathers) — sparkly, large-grained; on the snow surface or buried under more
  recent layers; the winter equivalent of dew.

- *Melt-freeze snow* (corn snow, spring snow, wet snow) — found on the surface or buried; forms from
  repeated melting and freezing of the snowpack.

**Persistent weak layers** — Weak layers that keep producing avalanches for days or weeks after a

storm. Some weak layers stabilize quickly after a storm; others — most notably faceted snow, depth
hoar, and surface hoar — take much longer, which is why persistent weak layers cause most avalanche
accidents (the danger can linger for days, just waiting for a trigger). The mere presence of a
persistent weak layer doesn't necessarily mean danger, but if it also produces unstable test results
or has caused recent avalanche activity, avoid avalanche terrain where those conditions exist.

**Probe** — A metal rod used to probe through avalanche debris for buried victims. Avalanche probes

are a backcountry must, and can knock precious minutes off rescue time. Collapsible probes assemble
quickly, are longer and slide through snow more easily than ski-pole probes, and are lightweight and
compact in a pack. You'll first search for a buried victim with a beacon, but as you close in, a probe
helps pinpoint their exact location so you can dig straight to them.

**Propagation** — The spontaneous spreading of a crack within the snowpack, without any added external

force. Slab avalanches occur when a crack propagates through a weak layer of snow underneath a slab on
a steep slope; propagation can spread extremely fast, letting huge slabs seemingly release
instantaneously. The propagation potential of a particular slab and weak layer determines how large a
triggered avalanche can become, and also whether avalanches can be triggered from flatter terrain
connected to steeper slopes. Whether — and how far — a localized crack propagates depends on a complex
interaction of many snowpack properties, further complicated by how quickly snow itself changes. If
several snow profiles on a test slope show easy compression tests and propagating extended column
tests, high-quality shears, and a persistent weak layer with a critical grain type, size, and hardness
difference relative to the slab — and the same conditions turn up in multiple profiles on the same
slope — you can safely conclude the snowpack can both initiate and propagate a crack, meaning you
should avoid all similar slopes steep enough to slide.

**Rain crust** — A clear layer of ice formed when rain falls on the snow surface and freezes; smoother

than sun crusts. Unlike sun crusts, rain crusts form uniformly on all aspects, but — like rain itself —
are highly elevation-dependent, since rain typically falls at lower elevations and turns to snow as you
climb through the freezing level.

**Remote trigger** — When an avalanche releases some distance from the trigger point — someone doesn't

need to be on the avalanche itself to trigger it. Especially in a snowpack with high propagation
potential, a person can start a fracture from a distance; it's common to remotely trigger from the
ridge above a slope, a gentler slope beside it, or especially a flat or gentle area below it. If you
remotely trigger an avalanche, the snowpack is extremely unstable, and route choices need extra care.

**Rime** — Supercooled water droplets that freeze onto exposed objects, forming icy deposits on the

windward side (and giving falling snowflakes a fuzzy appearance). It's the crunchy, rough,
popcorn-or-styrofoam-like snow plastered onto trees on windy mountaintops ("snow ghosts"), formed when
supercooled water in clouds touches a solid surface — snow, trees, chairlift towers — and freezes
instantly, growing spikes into the wind (unlike wind loading, where drifts form on the downwind side).

**Sastrugi** — Wind-eroded snow that looks rough, like frozen waves, usually found on windward slopes.

Wind erodes from the windward side of an obstacle and deposits on the lee side, leaving the eroded
snow with a rough, sand-blasted texture called sastrugi. Eroded snow is often assumed stable, since
wind erosion decreases the stress on buried weak layers underneath it — but that same wind is
depositing that snow on the lee slopes, increasing the load there, so sastrugi isn't always a sign of
stability elsewhere. Since you only see the surface texture, a buried weak layer could still be
lingering below, waiting for a trigger — treat all slopes as guilty until proven innocent by proper
stability tests.

**Settlement** — The slow deformation and densification of snow under gravity — not to be confused

with collapse. A fresh snowflake doesn't stay that way for long: as it ages its angular shape rounds
out and it forms bonds with its neighbors ("sintering"), and as sintering progresses the snow gets
denser and stronger — this is settlement. (People sometimes misuse "settlement" to describe the
catastrophic collapse of a snowpack, the loud "whumpf" — that's properly called collapse or
"whumpfing.") New, fluffy snow settles relatively quickly, within minutes to hours, faster at warm
temperatures than cold ones; settlement within new snow is generally a sign of stability, since it means
the new snow is rapidly gaining strength. As new snow settles it forms "settlement cones" around trees
and bushes, where the snow bonds to the vegetation, which props it up like a circus tent.

**Ski or slope cut** — A test where a rider rapidly crosses a slope to see if an avalanche initiates;

generally used by professional avalanche-mitigation teams, and requires training, experience and
judgment to use safely in the backcountry. It works well on thin, soft slabs, but not on deeper or
harder ones (and shouldn't be used there), and should only be performed on very small avalanche paths
or test slopes with minimal consequence if they do slide.

**Slab** — A relatively cohesive snowpack layer. When stronger snow overlies weaker snow, that's a

slab — or, as avalanche researcher Karl Birkeland puts it, "a slab is when you have something sitting
on top of nothing." A slab can occur anywhere in the snowpack, but avalanche professionals usually mean
the layer that slides off the slope to create the avalanche.

**Snowpit & stability tests** — A snowpit is a pit dug vertically into the snowpack to observe layering

and run stability tests (also called a snow profile); though commonly called "stability tests," these
are really "instability tests" — used to search for possible instability, not to prove the snowpack is
stable (due to spatial variability, never let a good test result convince you the snowpack is safe).
Snowpits don't need to take a long time — one philosophy is that if your feet get cold, something's
wrong, and rarely spending more than 10 minutes per pit; since snow varies a lot from place to place,
several quick pits, averaged, beat one long, exhaustively documented pit. Make the hole about a ski's
width wide, shoveling out the downhill side rather than digging straight down, so you have room to
work; then feel the wall with bare hands as well as looking at it, to notice how weak layers crumble
away while strong layers stay put. Since it's difficult for humans to trigger avalanches more than
about 1.5 meters (5 feet) thick (unless triggered from a shallower spot), pits deeper than 1.5–2 meters
are rarely necessary unless you specifically know of a deeper weak layer. Where to dig matters more
than how: choose a slope representative of the one you're interested in, without putting yourself in
danger — often a small, low-consequence test slope, working into progressively more dangerous terrain
as each test gives you a green light. Avoid ridgelines (wind-affected snow), thick trees, compression
and tension zones, and places where people have compacted the snow; look instead for neutral, open,
mid-slope areas without wind effects, and consider probing around first to find a representative
average depth without wasting time digging in a bad spot. Simple stability tests don't require steep
terrain — slopes of 25 degrees or gentler can give good data, meaning you don't have to expose yourself
to danger to collect it. Two of the most common:

- *Extended column test (ECT)* — isolate a block 90 cm wide by 30 cm deep and tap on one side using the
  same loading taps as the compression test. Count how many taps it takes to fracture the block, and —
  more importantly — note whether the fracture propagates across the entire block. Any full-block
  propagation is a red flag regardless of how hard the taps were; without other information strongly
  suggesting the snow is stable, avoid slopes where ECTs are propagating.

- *Compression test (CT)* — isolate a small column (30 x 30 cm), lay a shovel blade flat on top, and
  tap progressively harder until the column fails: ten taps from the wrist, then ten from the elbow,
  then ten from the shoulder, letting your arm fall under its own weight rather than pushing. Easy
  taps are bad, hard taps are good — but even with hard taps, follow up with an ECT to check for full
  propagation.

It's best to take a class or find a mentor to learn these tests properly, and — more importantly — how
to interpret the results.

**Stepping down** — When a slab avalanche slides a short distance and breaks down into deeper weak

layers, forming a stair-step pattern on the bed surface. With multiple weak layers in the snowpack, a
smaller, shallower avalanche can trigger a much larger and more dangerous one in a deeper layer —
especially dangerous because a person can trigger the small avalanche and then find themselves caught
in the bigger one. If a snowpack has the potential to step down into deeper layers, dial back your
exposure accordingly.

**Sun crust** — A snow layer melted by sun and subsequently refrozen. A frozen sun crust sometimes

forms a hard bed surface for future avalanches, though not always; when new snow falls on it, it may
produce short-lived loose snow avalanches, and over time small facets can form near the crust that
become a weak layer for future slab avalanches. Sun crusts only form on sunny slopes — mostly
southeast, south, southwest and west aspects at mid latitudes in the Northern Hemisphere (more
uniformly across aspects at tropical and arctic latitudes) — with many forming during a season, though
most don't become an avalanche concern. When new snow falls on a sun crust, check whether the crust is
wet or frozen first: wet, and the new snow will likely stick with no immediate problem; frozen, and the
new snow won't bond well. *Looks like:* shiny, slightly rough surface.

**Surface hoar** — Feathery crystals that form on the snow surface during clear, calm conditions —

essentially frozen dew — forming a persistent weak layer once buried. It's the frost you scrape off a
windshield in the morning, grown on the snow surface instead; it grows during clear, humid, calm
conditions, and once buried is a particularly thin, fragile, and persistent weak layer responsible for
a number of avalanche deaths each season. It's an especially tricky weak layer because it can form very
quickly — one calm, clear night is enough to deposit a thin layer — and once buried it's very thin and
hard to detect, yet very weak, forming in a complex, hard-to-predict pattern (only above a certain
elevation, or below one where cold humid air pools, or in a "bathtub ring" band where thin clouds sit
in a confined valley). It forms on open slopes rather than in trees, and because it's fragile, any
small disturbance — especially wind — can destroy it in one spot but leave it in another, making it
"pockety." It's often invisible in a snowpit wall, revealing itself only when you get a clean shear and
look at the bottom of the block for flat, feathery, sparkly crystals.
&nbsp;
*How it forms:* on a clear night, snow radiates heat away and the surface gets very cold; since warm

air holds more water vapor than cold air, vapor from the air above condenses onto the cold surface —
frozen dew. (In arctic latitudes, the mid-winter sun is so weak that surface hoar can grow all day,
even in sunlight.) The second ingredient is humid air — relative humidity rises either from added
moisture (left over after a storm, or near streams) or from falling temperature, and as air cools on a
clear, calm night it becomes more humid, often pooling in valley bottoms and basins — exactly where
surface hoar tends to form. The third ingredient is calm air: too much wind destroys the fragile
crystals and prevents cold air from pooling and humidifying; about 3 mph is considered ideal, fast
enough to keep bringing humid air to the surface without destroying it. In summary, surface hoar needs:
clear sky, no or very weak direct sun, calm or light wind (~3 mph), an open slope exposed to clear sky,
and humid air.
&nbsp;
*Distribution:* surface hoar doesn't grow under evergreen trees, whose branches disrupt back-radiation,

but grows fine in sparse aspen groves that don't block much radiation. Because cool air is more humid
and pools low, surface hoar tends to form more at lower elevations and in basin bottoms than on ridges
and summits — counterintuitively, more danger at lower elevations than higher ones, which surprises
people used to the usual pattern. Thick fog prevents it from forming (the surface needs a clear sky),
but thin fog lets it grow readily; if the fog layer is thick enough to block the valley floor, surface
hoar can still form along the top of the fog layer, creating a "bathtub ring" of surface hoar — the same
effect often seen along the top of a low stratus layer with mountaintops poking through. Once formed,
it's very fragile, and even light wind can blow or sublimate it away, so once buried it can be
devilishly patchy — a snowpit in one spot might show nothing while one ten feet away shows a fragile
layer. It's much more common in maritime climates (needs humid air) than continental ones, and grows
especially thick in high-latitude climates like Alaska, where the sun is too weak in mid-winter to melt
it off during the day.
&nbsp;
*Mechanical properties:* surface hoar may be the perfect avalanche weak layer — thin, very weak,

notoriously persistent, and it commonly forms on hard (and slippery) bed surfaces; thin weak layers
also tend to fail more easily since shear deformation concentrates into a small area. It can fail in
collapse if new snow buries it slowly enough that the crystals remain standing like columns — then one
thump critically loads them and they all collapse at once (like the classic trick of standing on an
empty beer can without crushing it, until one tap of a finger crushes it instantly). This is probably
the most common failure mode for surface hoar and other persistent weak layers: often the first or
second storm on top doesn't add enough weight, but the third or fourth finally tips it over. It can
also fail in shear if the first snowfall lays the crystals over on their side, leaving a paper-thin,
poorly bonded discontinuity — though laid-over crystals tend to bond up faster than standing ones.
&nbsp;
*Types:* surface hoar crystals come in roughly three forms — needles, feathers, and wedges — with

danger and persistence increasing in that order (wedges are worst). Needles form at very cold
temperatures (below -21°C), are tiny, and are less persistent, not forming thick layers. Feathers form
at more normal temperatures, are persistent, but lay down more easily than wedges. Wedges also form at
normal temperatures but are very persistent and tend to remain upright. Instabilities on a surface hoar
layer commonly last a week or two, and in the cold snowpacks of Montana and Wyoming, avalanches have
been observed on a surface hoar layer four months after it was deposited. The best defense is to
carefully map where surface hoar exists *before* new snow buries it — every loading event afterward

takes on life-and-death importance. A particular trap: during a multi-day storm, the wind can die and
the sky clear for just a few hours overnight without you noticing, growing a thin layer of surface hoar
that gets buried by the next round of snow — the next day's soft-slab avalanches, instead of calming
down as usual, keep going for days, and only digging reveals the culprit.
&nbsp;
*Best detection method:* shovel shear test or compression test, looking at the bottom of the block for

the crystals. *Forecasting considerations:* map the distribution before it's buried, and be suspicious

of it with every subsequent loading event — treat surface hoar as guilty until proven innocent.

**Terrain trap** — Terrain where the consequences of an avalanche are especially hazardous: a gully,

an abrupt transition, a path that runs out into trees, a crevasse field, or a cliff. The consequence of
an avalanche — trees (sometimes called the "giant bread slicer"), a large cliff, or a terrain trap — is
one of the most important factors in judging danger. A terrain trap is a sharply concave part of the
runout, such as a gully, abrupt transition, or crevasse, where debris piles up deeply; because
shoveling takes so long, deep burials have a very low survival rate — very few victims survive burials
deeper than about 5 feet. Even a small avalanche off the side of a gully can have deadly consequences.

**Trigger point** — The area where a trigger initiates an avalanche. When weather (usually wind, snow,

or rain) stresses the snowpack close to its breaking point, often just a small thump is enough to
initiate a fracture and shatter the whole slope like a pane of glass; because snow varies so much from
place to place, several people can sometimes cross a slope before one finally finds the trigger point.
The trigger point is often where the buried weak layer is especially weak, where stress on it is
especially great, or where the overlying slab is thinner or softer, letting a person's weight more
easily affect the weak layer below. In continental or intermountain snowpacks with faceted snow as the
weak layer, the trigger point is often near shallow, rocky areas or a ridge where the slab thins out;
on a recently wind-loaded slope, it's often where a thick layer of wind-drifted snow has overloaded a
steep section.

**Upside-down storm** — When a snowstorm deposits denser snow over less dense snow, rapidly creating a

slab/weak-layer combination. Most storms deposit denser snow on the bottom and lighter snow on top —
the way we like it — because most snow comes from cold fronts that start out warm and windy and end
cold and calm. But some storms deposit denser, stiffer snow on top of softer, fluffier snow — "upside
down" snow, sometimes called "slabby" or "punchy" because you punch through the surface slab into
softer snow below, making for difficult riding and trail-breaking. It also means a denser slab has been
recently deposited on a weaker layer, which should raise anyone's avalanche antennae, though most
instabilities within upside-down snow stabilize within a day or two. Common causes include warm
fronts, storms where the wind blows harder at the end than the beginning, or storms ending with an
unstable airmass that can produce a lot of graupel within instability showers.

**Weak interface** — A poor bond between two adjacent layers of snow. Avalanches usually fracture

within a discrete weak layer, but occasionally the fracture forms along a thin boundary between two
stronger layers — a common example is a slab sliding on an ice crust. *(See also Weak layer, under
Persistent weak layers.)*

**Whumpf** — When a weak layer's fracture causes an upper layer to collapse with a "whumpfing" sound —

an obvious sign of instability, and an actual technical term for the sound of a collapsing snowpack.
Most snowpacks collapse onto a persistent weak layer such as faceted snow, depth hoar, or surface hoar,
though whumpfing occasionally occurs on very wet snowpacks too. *(See also Collapse, above.)*

**Wind slab** — A cohesive layer of snow formed when wind deposits snow onto leeward terrain, often

smooth, rounded, and sometimes hollow-sounding. The slope's orientation relative to the wind is a huge
factor: wind erodes from the upwind side of an obstacle like a ridge and deposits on the downwind side,
and can deposit snow ten times faster than snowfall alone. This deposition happens most commonly on the
lee side of prominent upper-elevation terrain like ridges, peaks and passes ("top loading"), but wind
can also blow across a slope ("cross loading") or even up or down a slope — so it can deposit snow on
almost any aspect. Be suspicious of any steep slope with recent wind-drifted snow.

*Typical locations:* **Ridgetop loading** — ridge-top winds carry snow from the windward side to the

lee slope; cornices often form on steep ridgelines but may not appear on rounded ridge tops, and
windward slopes may show scoured snow or exposed rock/grass. **Side loading** — cross-slope winds

typically load gullies and chutes at any elevation, with higher areas scoured and low areas smoother.

Wind slabs are dangerous because wind grinds eroded snow into small, dense particles that pack into a
heavy layer wherever the wind slows down (the lee of an obstacle), easily overloading a buried weak
layer; strong wind can turn nice, fluffy powder into a dangerous wind slab within minutes, catching
people by surprise when very safe conditions suddenly turn dangerous; and wind slabs can form in
extremely localized areas, sometimes with only a few inches separating safe snow from dangerous snow —
"I was just walking along and suddenly the snow changed, cracking under my feet, and then the whole
slope let loose."

*Eroded snow* (sastrugi) looks sandblasted, scoured and scalloped; feels hard and difficult to

negotiate on skis, snowboard or snowmobile; and generally means weight has been *removed* from the

snowpack, making it more stable than before. *Deposited snow* (wind slabs, "pillows") looks smooth,

rounded, lens- or pillow-shaped, and chalky white; feels "slabby" — harder snow on top of softer snow —

and often sounds hollow, like a drum (the more drum-like, the more dangerous); watch for cracks
shooting away from you (the longer, the more dangerous) and for a harder surface layer that gives way
into softer snow below, felt easily with a ski pole or snowmobile track. It means weight has recently
been added to the snowpack — and if that's recent, on a steep slope without anchors, it usually means
danger.

If you find a wind slab on a steep slope: stop immediately and go no farther; back off on a big slope
and dig down to check how well the slab is bonded to the snow underneath; test the snow's response on a
few small, safe slopes; if the slab breaks away easily on those tests, don't cross larger slopes —
retreat or find another route that avoids wind slabs. If you absolutely must cross a dangerous slope,
stay on the extreme upper edge of the wind slab, wear a belay rope tied to a solid anchor, and hope the
crown fracture breaks at your feet instead of above you.
